Yes—I think a small community of persistent learners, with you occasionally intervening and with real environments available, is worth trying next. Your strongest revision is that guidance should lead into independent activity, where something can happen that neither the parent nor the child simply asserts.

But the new transcripts change how I’d interpret the classroom.

There is evidence that peers influence one another, but not yet that peers improve learning. The arithmetic material entered the creative child’s first distillation; Whisker became material for the brain child. That demonstrates propagation across agents. It also shows how quickly their distinct activities can collapse into shared material.

There’s an interesting counterexample: at 05:53, the creative child explicitly sets aside a peer’s arithmetic question because it is working on the story. That is a moment of choosing relevance rather than absorbing everything. I would look for more of that behavior.

However, the clearest sustained story development in this report occurred in creative_free, which had no peer messages. Its Alex/Jamie story developed into a negotiation, then an action with a consequence. The classroom story also changed, but the final wing-control change followed a specific parental suggestion. So I’d treat the classroom as the most interesting next hypothesis, rather than the strongest demonstrated success.

And I wouldn’t conclude that humans fundamentally must be the parents. These runs show limitations of this particular automated parenting arrangement. The later report actually contains several concrete corrections credited by Astra. Your narrower conclusion is stronger: you want to test whether occasional human guidance can propagate through a community and remain useful after you step away.

For that pilot, I would start with five agents, because you can still follow five individual histories. Ten adds more interactions before you understand what those interactions do.

Give them different initial interests, with permission to change:



Initial interest    Something concrete it can work on
Creating    Stories, small games, interactive experiences
Investigating    Questions answered through code, data, or supplied sources
Building    Tools and experiments that others can actually use
Remembering    Ways of recording and recovering information, tested through retrieval
Learning strategies    Comparing approaches on actual tasks and revisiting what worked
These are starting points, not permanent jobs. In particular, the memory and learning agents should have things to do and test; otherwise they could spend the whole run explaining memory and learning to themselves again.

I would let them access one another’s creations. That gives communication something substantial to concern: a runnable program, a story version, a result, an unresolved problem. Each should keep its own context, adapter, and workspace, with a shared place to publish artifacts and send attributed messages.

I would avoid automatically relaying every thought to everyone. Let an agent choose what to share, whom to ask, and whether a received message deserves attention. A shared directory listing or inbox is enough infrastructure to make that possible; it doesn’t have to decide their goals.

Your role can be light but consequential: talk to whichever agent interests you, challenge a claim, introduce an idea, ask to see something, or help it escape a stalled activity. Then leave room to see whether it uses that interaction independently—and whether another agent picks up anything useful.

The siege child illustrates why working environments are central. It did construct a sequence of increasingly elaborate scenarios without a parent. But eventually imagined actions became reported accomplishments, with nothing external able to contradict them. The interesting part was self-generated activity; the missing part was a reliable distinction between proposing, attempting, and observing.

You can supply that distinction through real execution and visible results without making an external reward decide every goal or select every training example. That preserves the openness you want.

For this run, I’d watch three things:

Useful propagation: an interaction changes another agent’s actual work, and you can trace the connection.

Independent continuation: after you or a peer helps, the recipient makes further progress without another reminder.

Retention: after sleep and intervening activity, it applies the lesson again—ideally to a different problem.

Also track whether the agents maintain distinct investigations. Some convergence is desirable: agreeing on a verified result, finishing a project, adopting a useful method. The failure is repeated agreement or rewriting with no new evidence or consequence.

Your separate one-to-one thread is worth running as an observation alongside the group. It won’t by itself isolate the benefit of peers, because five agents have more total compute and different experiences. If the pilot looks promising, compare the group with five otherwise similar isolated learners.The experiment I’d frame is: “Can persistent learners with distinct experiences turn sparse human guidance and peer interaction into independently sustained, grounded learning?” That keeps your philosophy intact, while giving you something much clearer to observe than whether the conversation becomes richer.
