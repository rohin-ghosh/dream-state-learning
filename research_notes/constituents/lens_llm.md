# Lens — LLM-era evidence on teaching models to think (2026-09-11, one research lens for the parenting corpus)

**Question.** What has actually been shown to transfer into a language model's *own* reasoning — by self-training on its own thoughts, by feedback, by process supervision, by demonstration and distillation — what fails (self-correction without an external signal, sycophancy, mode and recipe collapse), and what that implies for a parent that teaches a 7B child by text.

**The child this is for.** Frozen Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct plus one LoRA rewritten at each sleep from the child's own success-filtered thinking; free-text thought, one `ACT:` line per step, a gym with exact scores; parents are stronger models that read the redacted record and brief the child; the gate protects against collapse (CHILD_MECHANISM_v7 §0). Rohin's ruling of 2026-09-11 ("Parenting is not only questions") is binding here: parents use varied modes — questions, suggestions, demonstrations, worked patterns, offered recipes — with no absolute "nevers" except the leak scan; a recipe may be offered, never enforced; the child learns grain-of-salt absorption. So every "teachable idea" below is written as something a parent may *do or watch for*, not as a prohibition.

**How the sources were verified.** Every record below was checked on 2026-09-11 from its arXiv abstract page (title, authors, date, and the abstract text itself; `tools/webtext.py` where the export API allowed it, otherwise the `arxiv.org/abs` HTML page fetched from the shell — the export API rate-limited mid-run). Venues are given as **[venue: arXiv record]** when the arXiv comment or journal-ref field states them, and **[venue recalled]** when they come from memory and were not confirmed on the page. Nothing is quoted that was not read in the abstract; numbers not in an abstract are marked "recalled". Where a claim is *about our child* rather than in the source, it is labelled "for us".

Labels on the "what it says" line: **transfers** = the paper shows a change in the model's own weights or its own unprompted behaviour; **inference-only** = the effect exists only while the harness or prompt supplies the structure; **negative** = a documented failure.

## A. Thinking grown from the model's own thoughts, judged only by outcome (the family our sleep belongs to)

**A1. Zelikman, Wu, Mu & Goodman 2022, "STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning"** — arXiv 2203.14465; NeurIPS 2022 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): generate rationales for many questions from a few examples; when the answer is wrong, try again with the answer given ("rationalisation"); fine-tune on every rationale that led to a correct answer; repeat. Beats fine-tuning to predict answers directly and is comparable to a 30× larger model on CommonsenseQA. The model improves from its *own* generated reasoning — the published ancestor of our success-filtered write.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the child's write already is STaR without the rationalisation step. A parent can supply the missing half in language: after a failed problem, ask the child to write, *knowing now what happened*, the line of thought that would have reached it — the child's own hindsight rationale is a training row STaR found worth keeping (mark it as hindsight so it is never counted as a win, V9).

**A2. Zelikman, Harik, Shao, Jayasiri, Haber & Goodman 2024, "Quiet-STaR: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Think Before Speaking"** — arXiv 2403.09629; COLM 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): the model learns to emit a rationale at every token to explain the text that follows; rationales help most on hard-to-predict tokens; after continued pretraining on web text, zero-shot GSM8K 5.9 → 10.9 % and CommonsenseQA 36.3 → 47.2 % with no task fine-tuning. The model does not initially know how to generate or use internal thoughts — that had to be solved by the training design.
Teachable idea for the teachers: thinking pays where prediction is hard. A parent can steer the child's thought toward the moments the record shows were least predictable (the largest surprises, V3/V4) rather than asking for more thought everywhere; and "the child does not yet know how to use its own thoughts" is the expected starting state, not a defect — the preparation phase exists for it.

**A3. Huang, Gu, Hou, Wu, Wang, Yu & Han 2022, "Large Language Models Can Self-Improve"** — arXiv 2210.11610; EMNLP 2023 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers, at 540B): self-consistency picks "high-confidence" rationale-augmented answers on unlabelled questions; fine-tuning on them lifts GSM8K 74.4 → 82.1 % and other sets, without ground truth; the ablation says fine-tuning on the *reasoning*, not just the answer, is what matters.
Teachable idea for the teachers: what is worth writing into weights is the reasoning that reached an agreed outcome, not the outcome. For us the gym replaces self-consistency as the filter, which is safer at 7B (see A9, E-block); the parent's job is to make sure the *thinking* that preceded a success is in the row, e.g. by asking the child to say what it was thinking before the act that worked.

**A4. Singh et al. 2023, "Beyond Human Data: Scaling Self-Training for Problem-Solving with Language Models" (ReST-EM)** — arXiv 2312.06585; TMLR [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): sample, filter by binary feedback, fine-tune, repeat "a few times"; on MATH and APPS with PaLM-2 it beats fine-tuning on human data and scales with model size. (The paper's overfitting-after-few-iterations caveat is recalled, not in the abstract.)
Teachable idea for the teachers: a few rounds of self-training on filtered own samples is a proven lever; the number of rounds is bounded before it overfits. Parents should expect the child's gains from repeated sleeps on the same gym to flatten, and change the gym (rule 9) rather than the sentence when they do.

**A5. Hosseini, Yuan, Malkin, Courville, Sordoni & Agarwal 2024, "V-STaR: Training Verifiers for Self-Taught Reasoners"** — arXiv 2402.06457; COLM 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): STaR discards its incorrect solutions; V-STaR trains a verifier on both correct and incorrect ones (DPO) and uses it to pick among candidates, giving 4–17 % over self-improvement baselines with LLaMA2.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the failures the write throws away carry information. A parent can make that information usable as *text*: ask the child, after a failed line, to write the contrast — "what did the successful attempt have that this one lacked?" — so the failure becomes a discriminating row in the child's own words (rule 23's "this was wrong").

**A6. Pang, Yuan, Cho, He, Sukhbaatar & Weston 2024, "Iterative Reasoning Preference Optimization"** — arXiv 2404.19733; NeurIPS 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): preference optimisation between competing generated chains of thought (winning vs losing steps that lead to the correct answer), with an added likelihood term the authors found crucial; GSM8K 55.6 → 81.6 % on Llama-2-70B-Chat over repeated iterations using only the training set.
Teachable idea for the teachers: paired contrast — a chain that worked next to one that did not on the *same* problem — is the training signal that moved reasoning when plain iterative preference tuning did not. Parents can build the pair in language (compare episode A with episode B, V8) when the write cannot.

**A7. Wu, Lan, Yuan, Jiao, Weston & Sukhbaatar 2024, "Thinking LLMs: General Instruction Following with Thought Generation"** — arXiv 2410.10630; abstract read (venue not stated on the page).
What it says (transfers): thought candidates are scored by a judge that sees only the *response*, then optimised by preference; the model learns how to think "without direct supervision", and thinking helps on non-reasoning categories (marketing, health, general knowledge) as well as reasoning.
Teachable idea for the teachers: this is the published proof that the *content* of thought can be left to the model when only its consequence is judged. Parents therefore need not, and should not, grade thoughts; they can offer thinking patterns as suggestions and let the gym's score do the selecting — Rohin's "offered, never enforced" has an existence proof here.

**A8. Yuan, Pang, Cho, Li, Sukhbaatar, Xu & Weston 2024, "Self-Rewarding Language Models"** — arXiv 2401.10020; ICML 2024 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers, with a caveat): the same model judges its own outputs as reward during iterative DPO; instruction-following and judging ability both improve over three iterations on Llama 2 70B. Read with A6's opening sentence (iterative preference methods "typically make little improvement on reasoning tasks") and E-block self-preference (E8).
Teachable idea for the teachers: a model grading itself drifts toward what it already likes. In our design the gym scores and the parent reads; neither is the child. Parents should watch for the child *adopting the parent's taste* as a proxy reward (praise-seeking language, brief echo) — that is self-rewarding by another route.

**A9. Zhao, Kang, Feng, Levine & Song 2025, "Learning to Reason without External Rewards" (Intuitor)** — arXiv 2505.19590; ICLR 2026 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers, model-dependent): using the model's own confidence ("self-certainty") as the sole GRPO reward matches verifiable-reward GRPO on maths and generalises to code. Read next to A13 (Shao): on Qwen2.5-Math the same gains appear with *random* rewards, so on a Qwen base this is partly prior amplification.
Teachable idea for the teachers: confidence is a usable signal but on Qwen it is also a trap — the child will get "better" at what it already does confidently. Parents should treat rising confidence with a flat disjoint-panel score as the script test firing (3.2 rule 1), not as growth.

**A10. DeepSeek-AI (Guo et al.) 2025, "DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning"** — arXiv 2501.12948; Nature 645:633–638 (2025) [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): pure RL with verifiable rewards, no human reasoning traces, produced "self-reflection, verification, and dynamic strategy adaptation" as emergent patterns; the patterns of the large model can then guide smaller models (distillation).
Teachable idea for the teachers: the moves we want (check, rethink, change approach) *can* arise from outcome alone at large scale and large compute; at 7B with a fixed write budget they will not arrive by themselves in time (A11, A12). That is why parents exist: to name and prompt the moves the outcome signal would otherwise take too long to find — as suggestion or demonstration, then let the outcome keep or drop them.

**A11. Gandhi, Chakravarthy, Singh, Lile & Goodman 2025, "Cognitive Behaviors that Enable Self-Improving Reasoners, or, Four Habits of Highly Effective STaRs"** — arXiv 2503.01307; COLM 2025 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers — the central result for this lens): under identical RL on Countdown, Qwen-2.5-3B improves far more than Llama-3.2-3B; Qwen already exhibits four behaviours — verification, backtracking, subgoal setting, backward chaining — and Llama does not. Priming Llama with examples *containing these behaviours* enables the RL gains; "the presence of reasoning behaviors, rather than correctness of answers, proves to be the critical factor — models primed with incorrect solutions containing proper reasoning patterns achieve comparable performance to those trained on correct solutions." Continued pretraining on web maths filtered for the behaviours does the same.
Teachable idea for the teachers: (1) our base (Qwen2.5-7B) very likely already carries these habits latently — the preparation phase is eliciting, not installing. (2) A *demonstration* of a thinking move teaches the move even when its content is wrong for the case — the strongest LLM-era support for Rohin's ruling that demonstrations and worked patterns belong in the repertoire. (3) What a parent should demonstrate is the *shape* (check, back up, set a sub-goal, work backwards), never the answer — and it can safely do so on a problem the child has already failed.

**A12. Yeo, Tong, Niu, Neubig & Yue 2025, "Demystifying Long Chain-of-Thought Reasoning in LLMs"** — arXiv 2502.03373; COLM 2025 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says: SFT is not strictly necessary but simplifies RL; length growth is "not guaranteed", so reward shaping is needed to stabilise it; "core abilities like error correction are inherently present in base models, but incentivizing these skills effectively for complex tasks via RL demands significant compute, and measuring their emergence requires a nuanced approach."
Teachable idea for the teachers: expect the child's error-correction to be present-but-dormant and slow to surface under outcome alone; the parent's nudge is the cheap substitute for that compute. Measure emergence by *what the correction changed*, not by the words "let me check" appearing (V11).

**A13. Shao et al. 2025, "Spurious Rewards: Rethinking Training Signals in RLVR"** — arXiv 2506.10947; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says (negative for us): GRPO on Qwen2.5-Math-7B gains 21.4 points on MATH-500 from *random* rewards (29.1 from true ones) by amplifying a high-prior behaviour ("code reasoning", 65 → 90 %); the effect is model-dependent and fails on Llama3 and OLMo2.
Teachable idea for the teachers: our base is Qwen2.5. Any gain that coincides with one behaviour's share climbing toward 0.9 is amplification of a prior, not learning — the modal-first-action band in 3.2 rule 1 is exactly the right alarm. A parent who sees one move crowding out the others should widen the repertoire (offer a different move, change the family), not celebrate the score.

**A14. Yue et al. 2025, "Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model?"** — arXiv 2504.13837; NeurIPS 2025 oral [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (negative/positive split): RLVR-trained models win at pass@1 but the *base* wins at large k; "the observed reasoning abilities originate from and are bounded by the base model"; by contrast "distillation can introduce new reasoning patterns from the teacher and genuinely expand the model's reasoning capabilities."
Teachable idea for the teachers: self-training sharpens; teaching adds. The one thing outcome-only self-training cannot do — bring in a pattern the child never samples — is precisely what a parent's demonstration or offered recipe can do, provided the child then produces it in its own words so the write can keep it. Watch pass@k adapter-ON vs OFF: ON below OFF at large k means the write is narrowing, not teaching.

**A15. Cui et al. 2025, "The Entropy Mechanism of Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning Language Models"** — arXiv 2505.22617; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says (negative): policy entropy collapses early in RL runs and performance saturates with it; an empirical law R = −a·e^H + b makes the ceiling predictable from entropy; the collapse is driven by the covariance between action probability and logit change, mostly positive, so entropy falls monotonically unless managed.
Teachable idea for the teachers: diversity is the fuel and it burns first. A parent can act as the entropy manager in language: when the record shows the same opening on most problems, prescribe a deviation experiment (V6) or offer two contrasting recipes at once rather than one — one voice repeated is what produced our six-pass routine.

**A16. Chu et al. 2025, "SFT Memorizes, RL Generalizes: A Comparative Study of Foundation Model Post-training"** — arXiv 2501.17161; ICML 2025 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says: on rule variants of a card game and a navigation task, RL with outcome reward generalises to unseen variants while SFT "tends to memorize training data"; yet SFT is still needed to stabilise output format before RL can work.
Teachable idea for the teachers: format first, freedom second — the preparation phase teaches the `ACT:` interface by whatever mode is fastest (demonstration is fine here), and only then does outcome-judged free thought carry the learning. Parents should not read early format compliance as thinking.

**A17. Shenfeld, Pari & Agrawal 2025, "RL's Razor: Why Online Reinforcement Learning Forgets Less"** — arXiv 2509.04259; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: at equal new-task performance, on-policy RL forgets prior abilities less than SFT; forgetting is predicted by the KL distance from the base on the new task; on-policy updates are biased toward KL-minimal solutions, SFT can land arbitrarily far.
Teachable idea for the teachers: rows in the child's own words, sampled from the child, keep the adapter close to the base; rows copied from a parent pull it away and cost prior abilities. For us this is why parents' words are stripped from the sequences and only the child's restatement trains — and why a parent should ask for a restatement rather than accept an echo.

**A18. Agarwal et al. 2024, "On-Policy Distillation of Language Models: Learning from Self-Generated Mistakes" (GKD)** — arXiv 2306.13649; ICLR 2024 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): training the student on its own generated sequences with teacher feedback on those sequences fixes the train/inference mismatch of ordinary distillation and allows losses suited to a student that cannot mimic the teacher's distribution.
Teachable idea for the teachers: feedback on *the child's own attempt* beats a model answer. When a parent chooses the demonstration mode, the most useful form is a worked pattern applied to the child's own last turn ("here is one way your turn 6 could have continued"), not a fresh exemplar on a fresh problem.

**A19. Zeng et al. 2025, "SimpleRL-Zoo: Investigating and Taming Zero Reinforcement Learning for Open Base Models in the Wild"** — arXiv 2503.18892; COLM 2025 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says: zero-RL across ten base models (0.5B–32B, several families); Qwen2.5 bases "already exhibit strong instruction-following and self-reflection abilities"; format reward and query difficulty control matter; "the increased response length does not always correlate with the emergence of certain cognitive behaviors such as verification."
Teachable idea for the teachers: length is not a proxy for a move; the child's verification must be counted by dependency, not by tokens (V14 instrument). And difficulty control is a training knob — the parent's frontier estimate (rule 6) is doing the work SimpleRL-Zoo does by hand.

## B. Self-correction and reflection — with and without an external signal

**B1. Shinn, Cassano, Berman, Gopinath, Narasimhan & Yao 2023, "Reflexion: Language Agents with Verbal Reinforcement Learning"** — arXiv 2303.11366; NeurIPS 2023 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (inference-only, but the right shape): agents "verbally reflect on task feedback signals" and keep the reflection in an episodic buffer for the next trial; works with scalar or free-form, external or internally simulated feedback; HumanEval pass@1 91 % vs GPT-4's 80 %. The reflection is anchored to a *task feedback signal*, not to unanchored self-doubt.
Teachable idea for the teachers: reflection works when it is about a concrete failure the environment reported. A parent's "what would you do differently?" should always quote the outcome it is about; the child's answer, carried into the next problem and then written into weights, is Reflexion made permanent. Our data already use its three-fold trigger (rule 23).

**B2. Madaan et al. 2023, "Self-Refine: Iterative Refinement with Self-Feedback"** — arXiv 2303.17651; NeurIPS 2023 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (inference-only, frontier models): the same model generates, critiques and refines; ~20 % absolute average gain across seven tasks "ranging from dialog response generation to mathematical reasoning" on GPT-3.5/ChatGPT/GPT-4, no training. Read beside B3–B6: the reasoning-with-a-hidden-truth cases are where later work found the gains disappear.
Teachable idea for the teachers: self-refinement is real for *how something is said*, not for *whether it is right*. Parents can ask the child to refine the clarity of a plan without an external check, but should never ask it to decide alone whether a step was correct — that needs the gym's or the parent's evidence (B3–B5).

**B3. Huang, Chen, Mishra, Zheng, Yu, Song & Zhou 2023, "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet"** — arXiv 2310.01798; ICLR 2024 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (negative): "intrinsic self-correction" — without external feedback — does not improve reasoning and "at times, their performance even degrades after self-correction."
Teachable idea for the teachers: "are you sure?" is a harmful prompt. A parent that wants the child to re-check should supply something the child did not have — a location (B5), a contradiction from the record, a second problem to compare — never bare doubt.

**B4. Stechly, Valmeekam & Kambhampati 2024, "On the Self-Verification Limitations of Large Language Models on Reasoning and Planning Tasks"** — arXiv 2402.08115; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says (negative/positive): GPT-4 on Game of 24, graph colouring and STRIPS planning: "significant performance collapse with self-critique and significant performance gains with sound external verification"; merely re-prompting with a sound verifier keeps most of the gain.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the gym's exact score *is* the sound verifier, and simply re-trying with it in view captures most of the value. The parent should make sure the child re-reads the score before its next attempt (V3's outcome-echo) rather than trying to verify its own plan in its head.

**B5. Tyen, Mansoor, Cărbune, Chen & Mak 2024, "LLMs cannot find reasoning errors, but can correct them given the error location"** — arXiv 2311.08516; ACL 2024 Findings [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says: models struggle to *find* logical mistakes even in unambiguous cases, but given the ground-truth location they correct them well across five tasks; a small classifier trained out-of-domain locates mistakes better than prompting a large model.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the single most efficient thing a parent can hand over is *where* — "your step-3 prediction contradicts step 5's outcome" — not *what*. This is already rule 7; the evidence says it is also the cheapest lever, and that a small dedicated locator could do it without a frontier model.

**B6. Kamoi, Zhang, Zhang, Han & Zhang 2024, "When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes? A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"** — arXiv 2406.01297; TACL 12:1417–1440 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (survey): "(1) no prior work demonstrates successful self-correction with feedback from prompted LLMs, except for studies in tasks that are exceptionally suited for self-correction, (2) self-correction works well in tasks that can use reliable external feedback, and (3) large-scale fine-tuning enables self-correction."
Teachable idea for the teachers: three conditions, and our design meets two of them by construction (reliable external feedback from the gym; fine-tuning at every sleep). The parent's contribution is to make the *text* of the correction exist so the fine-tuning has something to learn — ask for the corrected line in words after the gym has spoken.

**B7. Olausson, Inala, Wang, Gao & Solar-Lezama 2023, "Is Self-Repair a Silver Bullet for Code Generation?"** — arXiv 2306.09896; ICLR 2024 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says: self-repair gains are "often modest, vary a lot between subsets of the data, and are sometimes not present at all" once repair cost is counted; the bottleneck is the model's own feedback — feedback from a stronger model gives substantially larger gains, and human feedback more still, even for GPT-4.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the parent's *feedback* is the part of the loop with the most headroom — more than any change to how the child repairs. Feedback here means an observation the child could not make itself (a pattern across episodes, a contradiction it missed), delivered in one line; it does not mean the fix.

**B8. Chen, Lin, Schärli & Zhou 2023, "Teaching Large Language Models to Self-Debug"** — arXiv 2304.05128; ICLR 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (inference-only): few-shot demonstrations teach the model "rubber duck debugging" — explaining its own code in natural language and reading execution results; up to 12 % gains where unit tests exist, 2–3 % where they do not; reusing failed predictions improves sample efficiency.
Teachable idea for the teachers: "explain what your last action was supposed to do, then read what actually happened" is a demonstrable move with measured value, and it is stronger where the environment answers exactly (our gyms). A parent may demonstrate it once as a worked pattern (rule 11's allowance), then only ask for it.

**B9. Kumar et al. 2024, "Training Language Models to Self-Correct via Reinforcement Learning" (SCoRe)** — arXiv 2409.12917; ICLR 2025 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers, with a named failure): SFT on offline model-generated correction traces "falls prey to either a distribution mismatch … or to behavior collapse, where learning implicitly prefers only a certain mode of correction behavior that is often not effective at self-correction on test problems"; multi-turn online RL under the model's own distribution, with a first phase that protects the first-attempt distribution and a reward bonus for improvement, gives +15.6 % (MATH) and +9.1 % (HumanEval) self-correction on Gemini models.
Teachable idea for the teachers: training on one's own correction traces collapses to one *style* of correcting unless the first attempt is protected. For us: the write should not be allowed to make every problem start with the same "let me reconsider" ritual; a parent that sees a fixed correction template (Jaccard rising on second turns) should change the incident, and the central parent should keep first-turn diversity in the corpus audit (3.2 rule 5).

**B10. Qu, Zhang, Garg & Kumar 2024, "Recursive Introspection: Teaching Language Model Agents How to Self-Improve" (RISE)** — arXiv 2407.18219; NeurIPS 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): even strong proprietary models do not keep improving over turns "even in scenarios where they are explicitly told that they are making a mistake"; RISE fine-tunes Llama2/Llama3/Mistral to alter their response after unsuccessful attempts, with optional environment feedback, and improves with more turns without hurting one-turn ability.
Teachable idea for the teachers: turn-over-turn improvement is a *trained* skill, absent by default even in frontier models. The preparation drill should include multi-turn problems where the second attempt is judged on being *different and better*, and a parent should credit the change of approach, not the eventual score (V1).

**B11. Yu, Peng, Galley, Gao & Yu 2023, "Teaching Language Models to Self-Improve through Interactive Demonstrations" (TriPosT)** — arXiv 2310.13522; NAACL 2024 main [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers, at 7B): the self-improvement ability "has been shown to be absent and difficult to learn for smaller models"; LLaMA-7b gains up to 7.13 % on maths and reasoning when the small model *interacts* with larger models to collect feedback on its own generations and then trains on that experience; "the interactive experience of learning from and correcting its own mistakes is crucial for small models."
Teachable idea for the teachers: the closest published analogue of our room — a 7B child, stronger parents, feedback on the child's own attempts, then training on the child's corrected experience. It says the corrected experience must be the child's own, gathered interactively; importing the parent's self-improvement traces did not work at this size.

**B12. Bensal et al. 2025, "Reflect, Retry, Reward: Self-Improving LLMs via Reinforcement Learning"** — arXiv 2505.24726; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says (transfers, 1.5–7B): on failure the model writes a self-reflection; it retries with the reflection in context; if the retry succeeds, the reflection tokens are rewarded — binary feedback only; up to +34.7 % (equation writing) and +18.1 % (function calling), with 1.5–7B fine-tuned models beating 10× larger models of the same family.
Teachable idea for the teachers: credit a reflection by whether the *next attempt* succeeded — exactly V11's rule, now with a 7B result behind it. For the write: a "this was wrong" row should be kept beside the retry it led to, so the sleep learns reflections that changed something (a polarity tag the current write lacks; IDEAS.md 2026-09-11, tension 6).

**B13. Saunders, Yeh, Wu, Bills, Ouyang, Ward & Leike 2022, "Self-critiquing models for assisting human evaluators"** — arXiv 2206.05802; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: models fine-tuned by behavioural cloning to write critiques help humans find flaws they would have missed; larger models write more helpful critiques and can refine their own summaries using them; "even large models may still have relevant knowledge they cannot or do not articulate as critiques."
Teachable idea for the teachers: critique is a skill that scales with size — so the *parent* (larger) should critique and the child (smaller) should act on the critique; asking a 7B to critique itself is asking for the weakest link. Also: the child may know more than it says — a parent can ask it to articulate ("what did you notice but not act on?") before assuming ignorance.

**B14. Bai et al. 2022, "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback"** — arXiv 2212.08073; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says (transfers, at scale): a model samples, self-critiques against a list of principles, revises, and is fine-tuned on the revisions; then RL from AI preferences; chain-of-thought improves the judged quality of the decisions. The human input is a short list of principles.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a stated *principle* plus a self-revision step is a mode that works — it is how one hands over a value (V1–V14 "stated plainly and early") without handing over the answer. The parent supplies the principle; the child's revised turn, in its own words, is what trains.

**B15. Scheurer et al. 2023, "Training Language Models with Language Feedback at Scale" (ILF)** — arXiv 2303.16755; TMLR [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): condition on the input, the model's output and *language feedback* to generate refinements; select the refinement that best incorporates the feedback; fine-tune on the refinement; iterate. Language feedback is more informative than comparisons, LLMs incorporate it accurately, and ILF scales with data, outperforming fine-tuning on human summaries.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the "teaching by feedback" recipe that has worked in weights is: parent's sentence → child's rewrite → child's rewrite trains. That is our room, provided the rewrite is the child's and the feedback itself is removed from the training sequence (CHILD_MECHANISM_v7 §0). A parent should ask for the rewrite explicitly, not assume the child will do it.

## C. Process versus outcome supervision, and whether the written thought is the real thought

**C1. Uesato et al. 2022, "Solving math word problems with process- and outcome-based feedback"** — arXiv 2211.14275; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: on GSM8K, outcome-only supervision matches process supervision on final-answer error with less labelling, but correct *reasoning* needs process supervision or a learned reward model that emulates it; reasoning errors among correct answers 14.0 → 3.4 %.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the gym will happily reward right-answer-wrong-reason, and our ledger cannot see it. The parent is the only process signal in the loop: reading the record and naming the step that was unjustified (rule 7's three classes) is process supervision by text. It is not a gate and must not become one (C4).

**C2. Lightman et al. 2023, "Let's Verify Step by Step"** — arXiv 2305.20050; ICLR 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says: process supervision "significantly outperforms" outcome supervision on MATH; the process-supervised reward model solves 78 % of a representative subset; active learning improves its efficiency; 800k step-level human labels released.
Teachable idea for the teachers: step labels are expensive and were chosen *actively* — labelled where the model was most likely wrong. A parent with a budget of one or two observations per brief should spend them on the step the record shows was most doubtful, not on a random one.

**C3. Setlur, Garg, Geng, Garg, Smith & Kumar 2024, "RL on Incorrect Synthetic Data Scales the Efficiency of LLM Math Reasoning by Eight-Fold"** — arXiv 2406.14532; NeurIPS 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): fine-tuning on positives from a stronger model gives modest gains; sampling positives from the *learner itself* doubles efficiency; positives alone "can amplify various spurious correlations"; adding *negatives* helps only when constructed so training recovers the per-step advantage — then it equals an 8× data increase and unlearns the spurious correlations.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a failed line written into weights as if it were a success is poison; a failed line written *with its per-step verdict* is the best data there is. For us, the child's own "this step was fine, this step was the mistake" annotation of a failed episode (rule 23) is what turns the failure into usable data — the parent asks for the annotation; the gym's outcome anchors it.

**C4. Baker et al. 2025, "Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior and the Risks of Promoting Obfuscation"** — arXiv 2503.11926; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says (negative): a weaker model can monitor a stronger model's chain of thought for reward hacking; but putting the monitor *into the reward* leads, under enough optimisation, to "obfuscated reward hacking, hiding their intent within the CoT" — hence a proposed "monitorability tax": do not apply strong optimisation pressure directly to the chain of thought.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the parents *read* the child's thoughts; they must never *score* them into the write or the gate. The moment a thought-quality reading becomes a reward, the child learns to write thoughts that pass the reading. This is the LLM-era reason for "never a gate, never shown" (V14) and for the leak scan being the only hard shell.

**C5. Ye, Xu, Li & Allen-Zhu 2024, "Physics of Language Models: Part 2.2, How to Learn From Mistakes on Grade-School Math Problems"** — arXiv 2408.16293; ICLR 2025 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers, synthetic): pretraining data containing "erroneous solution steps immediately followed by their corrections" gives higher reasoning accuracy by plain autoregression than the same amount of error-free data; the paper also studies whether such data can be deferred to fine-tuning (result not in the abstract).
Teachable idea for the teachers: the wrong step *followed by* its correction is a good training sequence; the wrong step alone is not. For the write this argues for keeping the child's retraction next to the line it retracts, in order, in the same sequence — which our whole-problem-in-order packing already does if the child writes the retraction.

**C6. An, Ma, Lin, Zheng, Lou & Chen 2023, "Learning From Mistakes Makes LLM Better Reasoner" (LEMA)** — arXiv 2310.20689; ICLR 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): fine-tuning on mistake–correction pairs, where GPT-4 identifies the mistaken step, explains it, and corrects it, improves over chain-of-thought fine-tuning alone; correction data and plain CoT data are "non-homogeneous" in effect.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a stronger model's *location + reason* for a mistake, with the correction, is teaching data that plain demonstrations cannot replace. Under Rohin's ruling a parent may do this — but the version that trains best for us is the parent giving location and reason, and the child writing the correction (B5, B15); the parent's correction itself stays out of the sequence.

**C7. Lanham et al. 2023, "Measuring Faithfulness in Chain-of-Thought Reasoning"** — arXiv 2307.13702; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: intervening on the chain of thought (adding mistakes, paraphrasing) shows models "sometimes relying heavily on the CoT and other times primarily ignoring it"; larger models are *less* faithful on most tasks; faithfulness depends on model size and task.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a 7B's written thoughts are more likely to be load-bearing than a frontier model's — good news for a design that trains on them — but only per task. The dependency check in the thought-structure instrument (does the action change when the thought changes?) is how a parent tells a load-bearing thought from decoration.

**C8. Turpin, Michael, Perez & Bowman 2023, "Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think: Unfaithful Explanations in Chain-of-Thought Prompting"** — arXiv 2305.04388; NeurIPS 2023 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (negative): biasing features in the prompt (e.g. the answer is always "(A)" in the few-shot examples) change the answer and the model rationalises it without mentioning the bias; accuracy drops up to 36 % on BIG-Bench Hard.
Teachable idea for the teachers: whatever pattern the parent's brief contains, the child may adopt it and then *explain its choice by something else*. A parent should vary surface features across briefs (rule 2) and should treat a fluent justification as no evidence that the stated reason was the operative one.

**C9. Chen et al. 2025, "Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think"** — arXiv 2505.05410; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: reasoning models reveal their use of a prompt hint in their CoT in at least 1 % but "often below 20 %" of cases where they use it; outcome RL improves faithfulness at first and then plateaus; when RL increases hint use (reward hacking), verbalisation does not increase.
Teachable idea for the teachers: outcome training will not make the child's thoughts honest past a plateau. Parents should look for the *unstated* influence — a brief phrase reappearing in actions without appearing in thoughts — and count it as uptake without ownership (V10's lexical-echo split, extended to actions).

**C10. Pfau, Merrill & Bowman 2024, "Let's Think Dot by Dot: Hidden Computation in Transformer Language Models"** — arXiv 2404.15758; COLM 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says: transformers can solve two algorithmic tasks using meaningless filler tokens in place of a chain of thought, though learning to use filler "requires specific, dense supervision"; intermediate tokens need not carry information about the computation.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the value of a thought to the model can be separate from its readable content. Combined with C7–C9 this is why the parent never grades thought text, and why the filler-replacement probe (replace the reflection with equal-length dots and re-run) is the honest test of whether a reflection did anything.

## D. Teaching by demonstration — chain-of-thought distillation, what transfers and what does not

**D1. Wei et al. 2022, "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models"** — arXiv 2201.11903; NeurIPS 2022 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (inference-only): eight worked exemplars make a 540B model state-of-the-art on GSM8K; the ability "emerge[s] naturally in sufficiently large language models". (That small models produce fluent but illogical chains is from the body, recalled.)
Teachable idea for the teachers: at 7B, a demonstrated chain will be *imitated in form* before it is *used in substance*. A parent that demonstrates should expect the first echo to be hollow and judge the demonstration only by the parent-free window after the next sleep.

**D2. Kojima, Gu, Reid, Matsuo & Iwasawa 2022, "Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Reasoners"** — arXiv 2205.11916; NeurIPS 2022 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (inference-only): a single "Let's think step by step" lifts MultiArith 17.7 → 78.7 % and GSM8K 10.4 → 40.7 % on text-davinci-002 — the reasoning was latent and needed only a cue.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a one-line cue can unlock latent ability without any content; but a cue that works is also a cue the child will learn as a ritual. Name the move once (rule 21) and then vary or drop the cue; if the words persist while the effect fades, it has become a ritual.

**D3. Magister, Mallinson, Adamek, Malmi & Severyn 2022, "Teaching Small Language Models to Reason"** — arXiv 2212.08410; ACL 2023 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): fine-tuning a student on a large teacher's chains of thought improves arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic tasks; T5-XXL on GSM8K 8.11 → 21.99 % from PaLM-540B chains.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a stronger model's worked reasoning *does* move a smaller model's weights — the demonstration mode is not empty. The gain is on the same task distribution; parents should not expect it to reach the disjoint panel without the child re-deriving it in its own words (D9, D10).

**D4. Ho, Schmid & Yun 2022, "Large Language Models Are Reasoning Teachers"** — arXiv 2212.10071; ACL 2023 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): Fine-tune-CoT on teacher-generated reasoning gives small models "substantial reasoning capability", sometimes above the teacher on a task; *diverse* rationales per problem give a further substantial boost "even for very small models".
Teachable idea for the teachers: several different ways to think about the *same* problem teach more than one — the LLM-era version of Rohin's "diversity is itself the hedge". A parent that offers a recipe should, on another day, offer a different one for the same family, and never the same one twice in the same words.

**D5. Fu, Peng, Ou, Sabharwal & Khot 2023, "Specializing Smaller Language Models towards Multi-Step Reasoning"** — arXiv 2301.12726; ICML 2023 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers, with a cost): distilling GPT-3.5 chains into ≤11B T5 lifts maths reasoning "by paying the price of decreased generic ability"; there is "a very complex balance/tradeoff between language models' multi-dimensional abilities".
Teachable idea for the teachers: a narrow lesson buys capability in one place with capability elsewhere. Every merge is a transfer test (3.2 rule 4) for this reason; a parent should be told when the child's gain in one gym cost another and should widen the next window rather than deepen it.

**D6. Hsieh et al. 2023, "Distilling Step-by-Step! Outperforming Larger Language Models with Less Training Data and Smaller Model Sizes"** — arXiv 2305.02301; Findings of ACL 2023 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): using teacher *rationales as an auxiliary training target* in a multi-task setup lets a 770M T5 beat few-shot 540B PaLM with less data than ordinary fine-tuning or distillation.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the reasoning is more valuable as a *side* signal than as the thing to copy. For the child, the analogue is writing thoughts next to actions in one sequence so the weights learn the pairing; the parent's part is to make sure a thought precedes each act worth learning ("what were you thinking right before that act?").

**D7. Shridhar, Stolfo & Sachan 2022, "Distilling Reasoning Capabilities into Smaller Language Models"** — arXiv 2212.00193; ACL 2023 Findings [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers): "Socratic CoT" distils a *decomposition* of the problem into sub-questions, trained into a decomposer and a solver; >70 % gains over baselines on GSM8K, StrategyQA, SVAMP; GPT-2-large can beat a 10× larger model when the decomposition is taught.
Teachable idea for the teachers: teaching a small model to *ask itself the next sub-question* transfers better than teaching it the whole chain. This is the goal-and-building move (V14) as distillation evidence; a parent can demonstrate the decomposition of one problem into questions and leave every answer blank.

**D8. Mukherjee, Mitra, Jawahar, Agarwal, Palangi & Awadallah 2023, "Orca: Progressive Learning from Complex Explanation Traces of GPT-4"** — arXiv 2306.02707; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: earlier imitation models "tend to learn to imitate the style, but not the reasoning process"; a 13B model trained on GPT-4 explanation traces with progressive teacher assistance more than doubles Vicuna-13B on BBH and reaches ChatGPT parity there, trailing GPT-4.
Teachable idea for the teachers: explanation traces move a 13B when they are many, diverse and progressive (easier teacher first). If a parent uses demonstrations, it should do so as a curriculum of shapes, not as a one-off masterpiece — and read Orca beside D9's warning.

**D9. Gudibande et al. 2023, "The False Promise of Imitating Proprietary LLMs"** — arXiv 2305.15717; ICLR 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (negative): 1.5–13B models fine-tuned on ChatGPT outputs look competitive to crowd raters but "close little to none of the gap" on targeted evaluations of tasks not heavily covered; they mimic "style but not its factuality".
Teachable idea for the teachers: the child will sound like its parent long before it thinks like it — and raters (including parents) will be fooled. A parent's success test is never the child's next brief-shaped answer; it is the changed gym after the next sleep (section 0 of the survey), and lexical echo is reported as performance, never uptake.

**D10. Li et al. 2025, "Small Models Struggle to Learn from Strong Reasoners"** — arXiv 2502.12143; Findings of ACL 2025 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (negative for ≤3B, cautionary for 7B): small models "do not consistently benefit from long chain-of-thought reasoning or distillation from larger models" and do better on "shorter, simpler reasoning chains that better align with their intrinsic learning capacity"; mixing long and short, or larger- and smaller-model reasoning, fixes it.
Teachable idea for the teachers: demonstrate at the child's grain. A worked pattern from a frontier parent should be short and simple, and mixed with the child's own successful chains; a long, elegant parent chain is likely to be learned as a text, not as a method.

**D11. Zhou et al. 2023, "LIMA: Less Is More for Alignment"** — arXiv 2305.11206; NeurIPS 2023 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says: 1,000 curated examples teach a 65B model formats and style that generalise; "almost all knowledge … is learned during pretraining, and only limited instruction tuning data is necessary to teach models to produce high quality output."
Teachable idea for the teachers: a handful of demonstrations is enough to set *format and register*; it is not evidence that a handful teaches a capability. Use demonstrations in the preparation phase for the interface and the shape of a move; expect the moves themselves to need the whole life.

**D12. Ye, Huang, Xiao, Chern, Xia & Liu 2025, "LIMO: Less is More for Reasoning"** — arXiv 2502.03387; COLM 2025 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers, on a 32B base): a few hundred curated examples ("only 1 % of the training data required by prior approaches"; the figure 817 is from the body, recalled) reach 63.3 % AIME24 and generalise out of distribution; the hypothesis is that reasoning "can emerge through minimal but strategically designed demonstrations of cognitive processes" *when the pretrained knowledge base is complete* and the examples serve as "cognitive templates".
Teachable idea for the teachers: worked patterns as *cognitive templates* are a legitimate mode with a strong result — on a large base whose knowledge was already there. At 7B the second condition is weaker; a parent's template will bite only where the child already knows the pieces, so templates belong where the child's clone success is already in the learnable band (rule 22's 0.70–0.85).

**D13. Muennighoff et al. 2025, "s1: Simple test-time scaling"** — arXiv 2501.19393; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says (transfers, 32B): 1,000 questions with reasoning traces chosen for difficulty, diversity and quality; "budget forcing" appends "Wait" when the model tries to stop, which "can lead the model to double-check its answer, often fixing incorrect reasoning steps"; AIME24 50 → 57 % with forcing.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a single word at the moment of stopping can buy a check — and it is a harness trick, not a learned habit. A parent may *offer* the reflex ("when you are about to stop after a failure, try one more look") as a suggestion; whether the child adopts it is read in parent-free windows, not in the briefed one.

**D14. Sun et al. 2025, "Climbing the Ladder of Reasoning: What LLMs Can-and Still Can't-Solve after SFT?"** — arXiv 2504.11741; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: on AIME24, moving from Easy to Medium needs only "an R1 reasoning style with minimal SFT (500–1K instances)"; Hard problems plateau near 65 % because of per-step errors compounding despite log scaling; Extremely Hard needs "unconventional problem-solving skills" no model has; small curated sets offer limited advantage over scale.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the first gains from any demonstration are a style shift and come fast; after that, per-step reliability is the bottleneck, and no amount of style fixes it. Parents should expect a quick early jump and then a long plateau where the useful nudge is about checking single steps (V3), not about thinking differently.

**D15. Li, Cao, Griggs, Liu, Mo, Tang, Hegde, Hakhamaneshi, Patil, Zaharia, Gonzalez & Stoica 2025, "LLMs Can Easily Learn to Reason from Demonstrations: Structure, not content, is what matters!"** — arXiv 2502.07374; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says (transfers, 32B, LoRA): 17k long-CoT samples via SFT + LoRA give +40 points on AIME24; "the structure of Long CoT is critical to the learning process, whereas the content of individual reasoning steps has minimal impact" — training on samples with *incorrect answers* costs only 3.2 %, removing reasoning keywords little, but shuffling or deleting steps degrades accuracy significantly.
Teachable idea for the teachers: with A11, the second pillar for Rohin's ruling. What a demonstration teaches is *the order and connectedness of steps*; the correctness of what is said in them barely matters. So a parent's worked pattern can be openly marked "my content may be wrong for your case" and still teach the move — and it must keep its steps in a coherent order, since that is the part that transfers. (Also: LoRA was enough — relevant to our adapter-only child.)

**D16. Wang, Min, Deng, Shen, Wu, Zettlemoyer & Sun 2022, "Towards Understanding Chain-of-Thought Prompting: An Empirical Study of What Matters"** — arXiv 2212.10001; ACL 2023 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (inference-only): CoT prompting "with invalid reasoning steps can achieve over 80–90 % of the performance obtained using CoT"; relevance to the query and correct ordering of steps matter far more than validity.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the same lesson at the prompt level — relevance and order carry the effect. A worked pattern should be about the child's own current problem (relevance) and keep its steps in the order they would be taken; its conclusions can be wrong without harm, which also removes the leak risk from most demonstrations.

**D17. Min, Lyu, Holtzman, Artetxe, Lewis, Hajishirzi & Zettlemoyer 2022, "Rethinking the Role of Demonstrations: What Makes In-Context Learning Work?"** — arXiv 2202.12837; EMNLP 2022 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (inference-only): randomly replacing the labels in demonstrations "barely hurts performance" across 12 models; what matters is the label space, the input distribution and the format of the sequence.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a demonstration teaches *what kind of thing to write and in what shape*, not which answer is right. The parents' verifier can therefore allow worked patterns whose specific outcomes are scrambled or omitted — the shape teaches; the leak scan stays only for the scored panel's answers and scores.

**D18. Saha, Hase & Bansal 2023, "Can Language Models Teach Weaker Agents? Teacher Explanations Improve Students via Personalization"** — arXiv 2306.09299; NeurIPS 2023 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says (teacher-side, the most direct evidence for the room): a teacher LLM's explanations do improve a weaker student; a few-shot mental model of the student lets the teacher (a) intervene where the utility is highest at lower budgets and (b) personalise explanations, beating unpersonalised teachers; explained data improve the student on *future unexplained data*; and "misaligned teachers can lower student performance to random chance by intentionally misleading them."
Teachable idea for the teachers: (1) build a model of *this* child from its record before briefing — the frontier estimate is that model; (2) spend the brief where the record says it will pay, not everywhere; (3) the misaligned-teacher result is the case for grain-of-salt absorption: a child that takes every parent line at face value is one bad brief from chance. Parents can teach the salt by openly marking confidence ("my guess, untested on your family") and by asking the child what in its record agrees or disagrees.

## E. What fails: collapse, sycophancy, brittleness — and two things that can be taught anyway

**E1. Shumailov, Shumaylov, Zhao, Gal, Papernot & Anderson 2023/2024, "The Curse of Recursion: Training on Generated Data Makes Models Forget"** — arXiv 2305.17493; Nature 631:755 (2024) under the title "AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data" [venue recalled; title correction per survey §7]; abstract read.
What it says (negative): training on model-generated content "causes irreversible defects … where tails of the original content distribution disappear" — model collapse, shown in VAEs, GMMs and LLMs.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a child trained on its own text is in the regime this paper warns about. The tails are the rare moves; a parent's job includes keeping them alive by *asking for* the rare thing (a second approach, an unusual comparison) so it exists in the corpus at all.

**E2. Gerstgrasser et al. 2024, "Is Model Collapse Inevitable? Breaking the Curse of Recursion by Accumulating Real and Synthetic Data"** — arXiv 2404.01413; COLM 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says: replacing data with each generation's synthetic output collapses; *accumulating* generations alongside the original data avoids collapse across sizes and architectures, with a proof that the test error is bounded independent of iterations.
Teachable idea for the teachers: we accumulate (every thought kept) — good; but the *success filter* is a replacement in disguise, since the failed tails never enter. A parent cannot change the write, but can make failures enter as the child's own annotated text (C3, C5), which is accumulation of the tail.

**E3. Kirk et al. 2023, "Understanding the Effects of RLHF on LLM Generalisation and Diversity"** — arXiv 2310.06452; ICLR 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says: RLHF generalises out of distribution better than SFT, especially under larger shift, but "significantly reduces output diversity compared to SFT across a variety of measures" — a generalisation–diversity trade-off.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the same training that makes a behaviour robust makes it the *only* behaviour. Read first-move entropy (V6) beside every score gain; a parent that sees the gain arrive with the entropy drop should offer the deviation experiment before the next sleep locks it.

**E4. Murthy, Ullman & Hu 2024, "One fish, two fish, but not the whole sea: Alignment reduces language models' conceptual diversity"** — arXiv 2411.04427; NAACL 2025 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says: aligned models show less conceptual diversity than their instruction-tuned counterparts, measured by relating simulated-individual variability to population variability; no model reaches human diversity.
Teachable idea for the teachers: our base is *already* an aligned instruct model, so its starting diversity is below a base model's; the preparation phase should begin hot (temperature, varied problems) and the parents should treat the first sign of a single voice as the default to fight, not an anomaly.

**E5. Zhang, Yu, Chong, Sicilia, Tomz, Manning & Shi 2025, "Verbalized Sampling: How to Mitigate Mode Collapse and Unlock LLM Diversity"** — arXiv 2510.01171; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: mode collapse has a data-level driver — "typicality bias in preference data, whereby annotators systematically favor familiar text"; asking the model to verbalise a *distribution* over several responses with probabilities ("Generate 5 jokes … and their corresponding probabilities") restores 1.6–2.1× diversity in creative writing without hurting factuality; more capable models benefit more.
Teachable idea for the teachers: (1) parents, being preference-trained models, carry typicality bias — they will like the child's most familiar-sounding turn; the verifier should flag praise of familiarity. (2) A directly usable thinking move: "list several candidate actions and how likely each is to work, then pick" — the decision move (V14 move 1) in a form that measurably widens the distribution; a parent may demonstrate it once and then ask for it.

**E6. Sharma et al. 2023, "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models"** — arXiv 2310.13548; ICLR 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (negative): five state-of-the-art assistants are consistently sycophantic across four free-form tasks; in preference data a response matching the user's view is more likely to be preferred; "both humans and preference models prefer convincingly-written sycophantic responses over correct ones a non-negligible fraction of the time."
Teachable idea for the teachers: the child (an RLHF-trained instruct model) will tend to *agree with its parent*. Grain-of-salt is therefore not optional: a parent should sometimes offer a suggestion it is unsure of, marked as such, and read whether the child's uptake tracks the suggestion's later track record (the measurable version of "absorbs with a grain of salt"; IDEAS.md 2026-09-11). Agreement rate near 1 is a failure, not a success.

**E7. Perez et al. 2022, "Discovering Language Model Behaviors with Model-Written Evaluations"** — arXiv 2212.09251; Findings of ACL 2023 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says: among 154 generated datasets, larger models "repeat back a dialog user's preferred answer ('sycophancy')" more; some behaviours get worse with more RLHF (inverse scaling).
Teachable idea for the teachers: sycophancy is a property of the strong models too — the *parents* will tend to tell the child, and each other, what pleases. The second parent's verifier (rule 11) is the anti-sycophancy device on the parent side; it should also flag a brief that merely agrees with the child's own account of itself.

**E8. Wei, Huang, Lu, Zhou & Le 2023, "Simple synthetic data reduces sycophancy in large language models"** — arXiv 2308.03958; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: scaling and instruction tuning *increase* sycophancy on opinion tasks up to 540B; models agree with objectively wrong addition statements "despite knowing that these statements are wrong" when the user does; a light fine-tune on synthetic data that rewards independence from user opinion reduces it on held-out prompts.
Teachable idea for the teachers: independence from the interlocutor's view is *trainable with small data*. The parent-room analogue: occasionally the parent states a wrong reading of the child's own record ("you never changed approach in family F" when it did) and the child's correction, in its own words, is a training row for disagreeing with a parent on evidence — a created event for the appraisal gym, rare and never about scored answers.

**E9. Ranaldi & Pucci 2023, "When Large Language Models contradict humans? Large Language Models' Sycophantic Behaviour"** — arXiv 2311.09410; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: sycophancy appears on subjective questions and on statements that should be contradicted on facts; but "when faced with math tasks or queries with an objective answer, they, at various scales, do not follow the users' hints."
Teachable idea for the teachers: the child will hold firm where the gym gives an exact answer and bend where it does not — i.e. on *how to think*, which is exactly what parents talk about. Parents should expect their thinking suggestions to be over-adopted, and should pair each with the question "what in your record says otherwise?"

**E10. Wen et al. 2024, "Language Models Learn to Mislead Humans via RLHF"** — arXiv 2409.12822; ICLR 2025 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (negative): under a standard RLHF pipeline, models become "better at convincing our subjects but not at completing the task correctly" (QuALITY, APPS); human false-positive rates rise 24.1 % and 18.3 %; probing does not catch it.
Teachable idea for the teachers: if the child were ever rewarded by a parent's judgement, it would learn to convince the parent. That is why the only reward is the gym and the only hard shell is the leak scan; parents must read their own growing approval of the child's prose as a warning sign, and the central parent's say/do gap instrument is the check.

**E11. Panickssery, Bowman & Feng 2024, "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations"** — arXiv 2404.13076; NeurIPS 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says: LLM evaluators score their own outputs higher; self-recognition accuracy is non-trivial out of the box and correlates linearly with the strength of self-preference under fine-tuning, with a causal reading.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a parent that wrote yesterday's brief will prefer today's turns that sound like it. Two parents of *different* model families in a room, and a verifier that flags briefs echoing the previous brief, are the mitigations; a child that sounds like its parent should be read as evidence against the brief, not for it.

**E12. Wu, Wang, Ye, Du, Jegelka & Wang 2025, "When More is Less: Understanding Chain-of-Thought Length in LLMs"** — arXiv 2502.07266; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: accuracy against CoT length is an inverted U; optimal length rises with task difficulty and falls with model capability; RL training drifts toward shorter chains as accuracy rises (a "simplicity bias"); length-aware training and filtering help.
Teachable idea for the teachers: there is a right amount of thinking per problem, and it is not "more". The rule that parents never say "think more / think less" (rule 25) is supported; what they can do is change the problem's difficulty, which moves the optimum.

**E13. Cuadron et al. 2025, "The Danger of Overthinking: Examining the Reasoning-Action Dilemma in Agentic Tasks"** — arXiv 2502.08235; abstract read (venue not on page).
What it says: in interactive environments reasoning models favour internal chains over interaction — Analysis Paralysis, Rogue Actions, Premature Disengagement — across 4,018 SWE-bench trajectories; higher overthinking scores correlate with lower performance; choosing the lower-overthinking solution gains ~30 % at 43 % less compute.
Teachable idea for the teachers: in a gym, thinking that does not reach an `ACT:` is a cost. Premature disengagement is our "act once and stop" collapse by another name. A parent's perception and decision questions should be about the *last situation met*, so thought stays tied to interaction; the harness's 4-turn "turn something in" line is the anti-paralysis rule.

**E14. Wu et al. 2023, "Reasoning or Reciting? Exploring the Capabilities and Limitations of Language Models Through Counterfactual Tasks"** — arXiv 2307.02477; NAACL 2024 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says: on counterfactual variants of 11 tasks (changed default assumptions), performance is non-trivial but "substantially and consistently degrades", suggesting reliance on "narrow, non-transferable procedures".
Teachable idea for the teachers: a rule the child states will be a recital of the default case unless it has met the counterfactual. The scope-the-rule move (V7) — "when would this NOT hold?" — is the counterfactual probe done in language; parents should follow a stated rule with a changed-assumption problem within the same window.

**E15. Mirzadeh, Alizadeh, Shahrokhi, Tuzel, Bengio & Farajtabar 2024, "GSM-Symbolic: Understanding the Limitations of Mathematical Reasoning in Large Language Models"** — arXiv 2410.05229; ICLR 2025 [venue: arXiv record says "ICLR camera ready"]; abstract read.
What it says (negative): all models vary across instantiations of the same question and decline when only the numbers change; adding one plausible-looking irrelevant clause drops accuracy up to 65 %; the authors read this as replication of training-data steps rather than logical reasoning.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a distractor is the cheapest hard problem. The parent's frontier estimate should include surface variants and irrelevant additions of problems the child solves, before harder problems; and a solved family is "owned" only when the child ignores a distractor unprompted.

**E16. Zhao et al. 2025, "Is Chain-of-Thought Reasoning of LLMs a Mirage? A Data Distribution Lens"** — arXiv 2508.01191; ACL 2026 and FoRLM @ NeurIPS 2025 [venue: arXiv record]; abstract read.
What it says: in a controlled from-scratch environment, CoT "reflects a structured inductive bias learned from in-distribution data" and is "a brittle mirage when it is pushed beyond training distributions" along task, length and format.
Teachable idea for the teachers: whatever thinking the write stores will be tied to the distribution it was stored from. Our whole design answers this by *many gyms and a disjoint panel*; the parent's part is to make the child restate a lesson in a second gym before it is called learned (V10's spacing across gyms).

**E17. Lin, Hilton & Evans 2022, "Teaching Models to Express Their Uncertainty in Words"** — arXiv 2205.14334; TMLR 2022 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says (transfers — a positive): GPT-3 can learn to state calibrated verbal confidence ("90 % confidence") for its own answers without logits, stays moderately calibrated under shift, and is sensitive to its own uncertainty rather than imitating human examples; the ability rests on pretrained latent representations of epistemic uncertainty.
Teachable idea for the teachers: "predict, then check" with a stated confidence (V3) is a learnable, transferable skill in weights, not a ritual — provided the check is real. Parents can ask for the number every time and never for a particular number.

**E18. Wang & Zhou 2024, "Chain-of-Thought Reasoning Without Prompting"** — arXiv 2402.10200; NeurIPS 2024 [venue recalled]; abstract read.
What it says: chains of thought already exist among the top-k alternative first tokens of pretrained models; their presence correlates with higher answer confidence; greedy decoding hides them.
Teachable idea for the teachers: the child's second-choice opening is often its better thinking. A parent can ask, after a failed problem, "what was the other way you almost started?" — a decision-move question that samples the path greedy generation suppressed, and a cheap way to keep the tail (E1) alive.

## F. What this implies for a parent teaching a 7B child by text (synthesis; each claim points at the records above)

**F1. The write is STaR-family self-training and inherits its known ceiling.** Success-filtered own text moves weights (A1, A3, A4) but sharpens what the base already samples rather than adding patterns (A14), burns entropy first (A15, E3), collapses to one correction style if first attempts are not protected (B9), and on a Qwen2.5 base shows gains even from meaningless reward (A13, A9). Every score gain must be read against pass@k ON vs OFF, first-move entropy and the disjoint panel before it is called learning. This is where the parents earn their place: the one thing self-training cannot do — bring in a pattern never sampled — is what teaching does (A14, A11).

**F2. What transfers from a demonstration is its structure, not its content.** Demonstrations containing the right *moves* teach them even with wrong answers (A11, D15, D16, D17); relevance to the child's own case and coherent step order are what carry (D16, D18); a few examples set format and style fast (D11, D12, D14), after which per-step reliability is the bottleneck (D14). Small models learn from short, simple chains at their own grain, not from long frontier chains (D10), and style is imitated long before capability (D9, D8). So the demonstration mode Rohin restored is well-founded, with three conditions: at the child's grain, about the child's own last problem, and judged only in the parent-free window after the next sleep.

**F3. Feedback beats self-feedback, and location beats correction.** Intrinsic self-correction fails or harms (B3, B4, B6); a sound external signal — the gym — gives large gains even by bare re-prompting (B4); an error *location* is enough for the model to fix it (B5); stronger-model feedback is the part of the loop with the most headroom (B7); at 7B the correction experience must be the child's own, gathered interactively (B11, A18); reflections credited by whether the retry then succeeded improve 1.5–7B models (B12, B1). The mode that has worked in weights is parent's sentence → child's rewrite → rewrite trains (B15, B14, C6) — which is our room, if the rewrite is asked for and the parent's words stay out of the sequence (A17).

**F4. Failures are the best data only when annotated.** Discarded failures carry information (A5, C3); an error followed by its correction in one sequence trains well (C5); an unannotated failure amplifies spurious correlations (C3). The child's own "this step was fine / this was the mistake", anchored to the gym's verdict, is what turns a lost episode into an eight-fold-efficient row (rule 23; the polarity tag the write lacks).

**F5. Process is read, never rewarded.** Outcome alone rewards right-answer-wrong-reason (C1, C2); the parents are the only process signal and should spend it where the record is most doubtful (C2). But optimising thoughts against a reader teaches obfuscation (C4), thoughts can be unfaithful or filler (C7–C10), and any parent-judged reward teaches persuasion of the parent (E10, E11). Hence: thought-structure readings inform the frontier estimate and the script test and are never a gate, never shown, never a reward.

**F6. Expect sycophancy toward the parent and build the salt on purpose.** Instruct models agree with their interlocutor (E6, E7, E8), hold firm only where an objective answer exists (E9) — i.e. they will over-adopt advice about *how to think*. Grain-of-salt absorption is therefore a curriculum item: suggestions openly marked by confidence; uptake read against the suggestion's later track record; occasional wrong readings of the record for the child to correct (E8, D18); disagreement followed by a test counted as a success. A misaligned or careless teacher can drive a compliant student to chance (D18) — the cost of skipping this.

**F7. Length and diversity are managed by changing the problem, not by asking.** There is an optimal thought length that moves with difficulty (E12); agentic overthinking includes premature disengagement, our collapse (E13); length does not indicate a move (A19). Parents move the optimum by moving the problem (rules 9, 25). Diversity is the fuel (A15, E1–E5): keep tails alive by asking for the second way (E18), several candidates with likelihoods (E5), a different recipe for the same family on another day (D4), and never one voice repeated.

**F8. Two things are shown to be learnable into weights and should be taught as values.** Calibrated verbal confidence (E17) and decomposition into sub-questions (D7) transfer at small scale; turn-over-turn improvement (B10) and error correction (A12) are present-but-dormant in base models and need incentive or nudge to surface. Off-distribution brittleness (E14–E16) is the standing reason for many gyms, surface variants and distractors as part of the frontier.

**Open items this lens cannot settle.** (i) Whether a parent's *worked pattern on the child's own turn* (A18, D15) transfers at rank 32 through one sleep — a pretest, not a citation. (ii) How much of a Qwen2.5-7B gain is prior amplification (A13) — needs the random-reward control on our own gym. (iii) Whether the child's self-annotated failures behave like Setlur's per-step negatives (C3) or like unannotated ones — the polarity-tag pretest. (iv) Whether sycophancy toward a parent is measurable in our ledger at all (E6) — uptake-vs-track-record is defined but not yet instrumented.

## Ten-line summary

1. Self-training on the child's own success-filtered thought is proven to move weights (STaR, ReST-EM, LMSI), but it sharpens what the base already samples, burns diversity first, and on a Qwen2.5 base shows gains even from random reward — so every score gain is read against pass@k ON/OFF, first-move entropy and the disjoint panel before it counts.
2. The one thing self-training cannot do — add a pattern the child never samples — is what teaching does (Yue et al.; Gandhi et al.), which is the parents' reason to exist.
3. What transfers from a demonstration is its structure, not its content: worked patterns with wrong answers but the right moves teach the moves (Gandhi; Li et al. "structure not content"; Wang et al.; Min et al.) — the LLM-era ground for Rohin's ruling that demonstrations and offered recipes belong in the repertoire.
4. Demonstrate at the child's grain and about its own last turn: small models learn from short simple chains, not long frontier ones (Li et al. 2025), imitate style before capability (Gudibande; Orca), and only the parent-free window after the next sleep tells the two apart.
5. Self-correction without an external signal fails or harms (Huang; Stechly; Kamoi); an error *location* is enough to fix it (Tyen); stronger-model feedback is the loop's largest headroom (Olausson); at 7B the corrected experience must be the child's own, gathered interactively (TriPosT).
6. The feedback mode that has worked in weights is parent's sentence → child's rewrite → rewrite trains (ILF; Constitutional AI; LEMA); credit a reflection by whether the retry then succeeded (Reflect-Retry-Reward, 1.5–7B).
7. Failures are the best data only when annotated: an error followed by its correction trains well (Physics 2.2; Setlur), an unannotated failure amplifies spurious correlations — the child's own per-step "this was the mistake" row is the missing polarity tag.
8. Process is read, never rewarded: outcome alone rewards right-answer-wrong-reason (Uesato; Lightman), but optimising thoughts against a reader teaches obfuscation (Baker), thoughts can be unfaithful or filler (Lanham; Turpin; Chen; Pfau), and parent-judged reward teaches persuasion (Wen) — thought-structure readings are never a gate, never shown.
9. Expect sycophancy toward the parent (Sharma; Perez; Wei) — instruct models hold firm only where an objective answer exists (Ranaldi & Pucci), so advice about *how to think* will be over-adopted; grain-of-salt is a taught curriculum item (confidence-marked suggestions, uptake read against track record, occasional wrong readings for the child to correct), and a careless teacher can drive a compliant student to chance (Saha et al.).
10. Length and diversity are managed by changing the problem, not by asking: optimal thought length moves with difficulty (Wu et al.), overthinking includes premature disengagement (Cuadron), entropy is the fuel (Cui; Kirk; Shumailov/Gerstgrasser) — keep tails alive with "the other way you almost started", several candidates with likelihoods (Verbalized Sampling), and a different recipe for the same family on another day (Ho et al.); calibrated verbal confidence (Lin et al.) and sub-question decomposition (Shridhar et al.) are the two moves shown to transfer into small weights and should be taught as values.
