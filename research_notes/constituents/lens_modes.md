# Lens: teaching modes beyond questioning — what the evidence says about mixing and sequencing them (2026-09-11, Fable's agent)

**Ruling this lens serves.** Rohin, 2026-09-11 (IDEAS.md, "Parenting is not only questions"): parents use varied modes — questions, suggestions, demonstrations, worked patterns, offered recipes, stories from the record — with no absolute "nevers" except the leak scan; a recipe may be offered, marked as opinion, never enforced; the child learns grain-of-salt absorption; the output is teachable ideas for the teachers of a 7B language-model child. Two facts about this child bear on every mode below: (a) only its own words reach its weights, so a parent's demonstration teaches nothing until the child re-says it — and when it does re-say it, verbatim, the weights store a recipe (the v6 six-pass lock-in); (b) a brief is in context for one wake, then gone, so "instruction" here is always short, always followed by practice, and always tested with the parent absent.

---

## 0. The question and the answer in one paragraph

Which mixture and sequencing of teaching modes does the evidence favour, and how should a parent vary them across a life so no single voice becomes a recipe? The literature does not crown a mode. It says, repeatedly and from several traditions, that the right mode depends on how much the learner already knows about *this* thing (the expertise reversal effect), that explicit statement and worked patterns win for novices and for domain-general habits, that struggle-first wins for domain content once the learner can generate attempts and the instruction that follows builds on those attempts, that questions alone teach as well as explanations do, that demonstration narrows exploration, that answer-giving creates dependence which shows only when the helper is withdrawn, that untrained tutors default to telling, and that varying the conditions of practice costs immediate performance and buys retention and transfer. The common shape is a *ladder with a hinge*: show or state early, ask in the middle, withdraw at the end, and move the hinge per lesson and per family by what the child now does unprompted — not by a global philosophy of teaching. The hedge against a single voice is not a ban on any mode but a measured rotation of modes, an audit of each parent's mode share, and a child taught to weigh every offering against its own record.

## 1. Sources, by mode

Each entry: one-line record (authors, year, venue, how verified); what it says; a teachable idea for the teachers.

### A. Direct and explicit instruction

**Rosenshine 2012, "Principles of Instruction: Research-Based Strategies That All Teachers Should Know", American Educator 36(1):12–19, 39 (ERIC EJ971753; condensed reprint in Education Digest 78(3), ERIC EJ1002984) [V, ERIC abstracts].**
What it says: ten principles drawn from cognitive science, studies of master teachers and research on cognitive supports, which the author says do not conflict. Among them: present new material in small steps with practice after each; ask many questions and check every learner's response; provide models; guide practice; keep the success rate high; give temporary scaffolds for difficult tasks; then extensive, successful, independent practice until automatic; and weekly and monthly review. A synthesis, not a trial.
Teachable idea for the teachers: a brief that introduces something new does one small thing, then the next problems practise it before anything else is added; the parent's "models" are the worked patterns of §B, temporary by construction; the "review" is a retrieval question weeks later, not a re-statement. The high-success-rate principle is the clone success band (0.70–0.85) already in the survey's Stage 1.

**Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, "Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work", Educational Psychologist 41(2):75–86 (DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1) [V].**
What it says: from cognitive-load theory and half a century of studies, minimally guided instruction is less effective and less efficient than strongly guided instruction; the advantage of guidance recedes only when learners have enough prior knowledge to guide themselves. The reply to commentaries (Sweller, Kirschner & Clark 2007, Educational Psychologist 42(2):115–121 [V]) insists on one-variable-at-a-time controlled comparisons and rejects reducing information in favour of teaching learners to find it.
Teachable idea: on a family the child has never met, the parent gives more than a question — a stated value, a named feature of the family, one worked pattern on a problem the child is not scored on — and moves to question-only when the child's own attempts show the internal guidance has arrived (unprompted moves in ≥ 3 of the last 8, the survey's criterion). "Minimal guidance from the first day" is the failure this paper documents; "questions only" is minimal guidance under another name for a child that cannot yet answer them.

**Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn 2007, "Scaffolding and Achievement in Problem-Based and Inquiry Learning: A Response to Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark (2006)", Educational Psychologist 42(2):99–107 [V]; Schmidt, Loyens, van Gog & Paas 2007, "Problem-Based Learning is Compatible with Human Cognitive Architecture", Educational Psychologist 42(2):91–97 [V].**
What they say: Kirschner et al. conflated problem-based and inquiry learning with unguided discovery; those approaches are heavily scaffolded, reduce load through scaffolding, and allow guidance to be adapted flexibly to the learner; they also serve goals beyond content (epistemic practices, self-directed learning). Both sides of the exchange agree that unguided novices learn badly; they disagree about whether "problem first" is unguided.
Teachable idea: the debate is about *when and how much*, not *whether*. The parent's question is never "instruction or discovery" but "how much structure does this child need on this family this week, and how do I remove it".

**Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich & Tenenbaum 2011, "Does discovery-based instruction enhance learning?", Journal of Educational Psychology 103(1):1–18 (DOI 10.1037/a0021017) [V, abstract via Semantic Scholar].**
What it says: two meta-analyses over 164 studies. Unassisted discovery lost to explicit instruction across 580 comparisons (d = −0.38); enhanced or assisted discovery beat other forms of instruction across 360 comparisons (d = 0.30). The authors' summary: unassisted discovery does not benefit learners, whereas feedback, worked examples, scaffolding and elicited explanations do.
Teachable idea: the four things that made discovery work — feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, elicited explanation — are four modes. The parent's job is to have all four in hand and to leave the child alone only *after* one of them has been used; "leave it to discover" is the losing arm.

### B. Worked examples, the expertise reversal effect, fading

**Sweller & Cooper 1985, "The Use of Worked Examples as a Substitute for Problem Solving in Learning Algebra", Cognition and Instruction 2(1):59–89 (DOI 10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3) [V].**
What it says: five experiments. Worked examples took much less time to process than conventional problems, and later problems of the same structure were then solved faster with fewer errors; both gains were specific to problems identical in structure to the examples. Novices hold general rules in only a few schemas; abstracting a general rule needs considerable practice across a wider range of schemas.
Teachable idea: a worked pattern shown once buys speed on the *same* structure and nothing beyond it — so a demonstration is always followed by problems whose surface differs, and a child that reproduces the pattern on a structurally different problem has learned the pattern, not the rule. This is the mechanism of the six-pass lock-in: one structure, thousands of exposures.

**Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003, "The Expertise Reversal Effect", Educational Psychologist 38(1):23–31 (DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep3801_4) [V]; Kalyuga 2007, "Expertise Reversal Effect and Its Implications for Learner-Tailored Instruction", Educational Psychology Review 19(4):509–539 [V, Springer abstract].**
What they say: instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners lose their effect and can harm more experienced learners; the 2007 review ties this to aptitude–treatment interaction research and to adaptive systems that switch technique as expertise grows.
Teachable idea: the same parent sentence is a help on Monday and a harm on Friday. Every mode has an expiry set by the child, not the calendar: a worked pattern or an offered recipe on a family the child already handles unprompted is over-scaffolding and is flagged by the verifier as such (the survey's rule 24 already flags "a move the child has produced unprompted for two windows"; extend the same flag to demonstrations and recipes per family).

**Renkl & Atkinson 2003, "Structuring the Transition From Example Study to Problem Solving in Cognitive Skill Acquisition", Educational Psychologist 38(1):15–22 (DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep3801_3) [V].**
What it says: worked examples beat problem solving early in skill acquisition and lose to it later; the authors propose a *fading* procedure in which problem-solving steps are progressively substituted into example study until the learner solves alone, and report evidence for it, with the open question of how to make the fade adaptive to the learner's prior knowledge.
Teachable idea: the fade is a ladder of completeness, not an on/off switch. For a gym family: (1) a complete worked pattern on an unscored problem; (2) a pattern with the last step missing — "what would you do here?"; (3) a pattern with only the first step given; (4) a question; (5) silence. Move one rung on success, back one on failure (Wood & Middleton's contingency, below).

**Wittwer & Renkl 2008, "Why Instructional Explanations Often Do Not Work", Educational Psychologist 43(1):49–64 (DOI 10.1080/00461520701756420) [V].**
What it says: explanations are the commonest instructional move and research shows they often do not contribute to learning; the framework gathers the conditions under which they do — chiefly that the learner must actively use the explanation, that it must be adapted to what the learner already knows, and that it should not replace the learner's own processing.
Teachable idea: an explanation in a brief counts only if the next problem forces the child to *use* it and the child's own sentence about it appears afterwards. "Explain and hope" is the default failure of every strong model acting as teacher; the verifier's check "answerable by copying the brief" is the right test.

**Rittle-Johnson & Star 2007, "Does comparing solution methods facilitate conceptual and procedural knowledge?", Journal of Educational Psychology 99(3):561–574 (DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.561) [V].**
What it says: 70 seventh-graders randomly assigned to compare two solution methods side by side or to reflect on the same methods one at a time; the compare group gained more procedural knowledge and flexibility, with comparable conceptual gains.
Teachable idea: when the parent shows a worked pattern, show *two* — the child's own approach from the record next to one alternative, and ask what differs and what that changes. Two patterns side by side are harder to copy as a recipe than one, and the comparison is the child's constructive work.

### C. Modelling, cognitive apprenticeship, scaffolding and its fade

**Collins, Brown & Newman 1987, "Cognitive Apprenticeship: Teaching the Craft of Reading, Writing, and Mathematics", BBN/Center for the Study of Reading Technical Report 403 (ERIC ED284181; 1989 book chapter in Resnick ed., Knowing, Learning, and Instruction) [V, ERIC abstract read; the method list below is recalled from the paper and not in the abstract].**
What it says: retool apprenticeship for cognitive skills; three exemplars (Palincsar & Brown's reciprocal teaching, Scardamalia & Bereiter's procedural facilitation, Schoenfeld's problem solving) develop both cognitive and metacognitive skill; a framework covers content, method, sequencing and the sociology of learning. The methods — modelling, coaching, scaffolding, fading, articulation, reflection, exploration — are recalled, not read here.
Teachable idea: *modelling* — the master thinking aloud while doing — is the first rung, and in this framework it is legitimate, not a leak. A parent may think aloud through one problem the child is not scored on, in the child's own move vocabulary, once per new family or new move, and then coach (question) and fade. Articulation is the child's own words in the TO PARENT channel; reflection is private time; exploration is the parent-free window.

**Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976, "The Role of Tutoring in Problem Solving", Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry 17(2):89–100 (DOI 10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x) [V record; the six scaffolding functions — recruitment, reduction in degrees of freedom, direction maintenance, marking critical features, frustration control, demonstration — are recalled].**
What it says: the tutor controls the parts of the task beyond the learner's reach so the learner can attend to the parts within it; demonstration is one of the six named functions.
Teachable idea: "demonstration" was in the original list of scaffolding functions. The survey's rule 20 forbade it for moves; under the ruling it returns as one function among six, used when the others have not moved the child, and marked so the child can name it ("this is me showing one way").

**Wood & Middleton 1975, "A Study of Assisted Problem-Solving", British Journal of Psychology 66(2):181–191 (DOI 10.1111/j.2044-8295.1975.tb01454.x) [V].**
What it says: twelve mothers helping their children assemble a construction toy; mothers who systematically changed their instructions on the basis of the child's response to the previous intervention — more help after failure, less after success — were the most likely to see the child succeed alone afterwards, and the most likely to concentrate on the child's "region of sensitivity to instruction".
Teachable idea: the contingent shift is the one rule that decides mode: after a failure, one rung *more* (question → part-pattern → full pattern); after a success, one rung *less*. The rung, not the sentence, is what the parent logs; the audit is whether the shifts were contingent.

**van de Pol, Volman & Beishuizen 2010, "Scaffolding in Teacher–Student Interaction: A Decade of Research", Educational Psychology Review 22(3):271–296 (DOI 10.1007/s10648-010-9127-6) [V].**
What it says: contingency, fading and transfer of responsibility are the three defining characteristics of scaffolding; descriptive studies are many, effectiveness studies few but positive; measurement is the main challenge.
Teachable idea: a mode counts as scaffolding only if all three are present — it was chosen by the child's last response, it will be withdrawn, and responsibility passes to the child. An offered recipe that is repeated every wake fails the second test whatever its content.

**Puntambekar & Hübscher 2005, "Tools for Scaffolding Students in a Complex Learning Environment: What Have We Gained and What Have We Missed?", Educational Psychologist 40(1):1–12 (DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep4001_1) [V].**
What it says: as "scaffolding" migrated from tutor–learner interaction to software tools and curricula, ongoing diagnosis, calibrated support and fading were dropped; they propose designing supports at several levels so the tool itself can be removed.
Teachable idea: the harness's fixed helps — the four-turn "turn something in" line, the brief's fixed shape — are scaffolds that never fade. Consider a per-life or per-stage removal of at least one (for example, the birth brief's move format once the moves are owned) and measure what happens with it gone.

**Reiser 2004, "Scaffolding Complex Learning: The Mechanisms of Structuring and Problematizing Student Work", Journal of the Learning Sciences 13(3):273–304 (DOI 10.1207/s15327809jls1303_2) [V].**
What it says: scaffolds work by two mechanisms that are sometimes in tension — *structuring* (guiding through components, supporting planning) and *problematizing* (making the learner engage with disciplinary complexity, which makes the task harder in the short term but more productive).
Teachable idea: two families of parent mode. Structuring modes (worked pattern, offered recipe, stated value) make the next problem easier; problematizing modes (a question at the boundary, a contrasting pair, a harder surface) make it harder. A healthy brief history alternates them; a parent that only structures produces a dependent child, one that only problematizes produces a stuck one.

### D. Guided discovery, productive failure, and the time for telling

**Kapur 2008, "Productive Failure", Cognition and Instruction 26(3):379–424 (DOI 10.1080/07370000802212669) [V]; Kapur 2014, "Productive Failure in Learning Math", Cognitive Science 38(5):1008–1022 (DOI 10.1111/cogs.12107) [V].**
What they say: 2008 — eleventh-graders solving ill-structured kinematics problems without support produced poor solutions but then outperformed the well-structured group on individual near- and far-transfer measures. 2014 — two randomised studies; both orders gave high procedural knowledge, but problem-solving-before-instruction gave greater conceptual understanding and transfer; the number of student-generated solutions predicted learning.
Teachable idea: on a new family, let the child attempt first for a round or two *before* any brief, and count its distinct approaches — that count is the predictor. Then the brief is built on those attempts. The instruction is not skipped; it is moved to after the struggle and made of the child's own material.

**Sinha & Kapur 2021, "When Problem Solving Followed by Instruction Works: Evidence for Productive Failure", Review of Educational Research 91(5):761–798 (DOI 10.3102/00346543211019105) [V].**
What it says: meta-analysis of 53 studies, 166 comparisons; problem-solving-first beats instruction-first by g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51], stronger (0.37–0.58) with high fidelity to productive-failure principles; the effect *reverses* for younger learners and for domain-general skills, where instruction-first wins.
Teachable idea: two schedules, not one. For a gym's content (which pass order, which potion), attempt first, instruction second. For the values and thinking moves (persist by changing, predict then check, decide, prune), state first, probe second. The child's "age" is per family: a fresh family is a younger learner.

**Loibl, Roll & Rummel 2017, "Towards a Theory of When and How Problem Solving Followed by Instruction Supports Learning", Educational Psychology Review 29(4):693–715 (DOI 10.1007/s10648-016-9379-x) [V, Springer abstract].**
What it says: a review of problem-solving-first designs finds they foster learning *only* when specific features are present — contrasting cases, or instruction that builds on the students' own solutions — and proposes the mechanisms (activating prior knowledge, awareness of knowledge gaps, recognising deep features).
Teachable idea: the two features are already parent moves: the contrasting pair from the child's record, and a brief at least half of whose lines cite the child's own attempts (the survey's rule 4). Struggle without either is just struggle.

**Schwartz & Bransford 1998, "A Time for Telling", Cognition and Instruction 16(4):475–522 (DOI 10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4) [V, ERIC EJ582423 abstract].**
What it says: three studies; a method that has learners analyse contrasting cases first prepares them to learn from a subsequent lecture or text; there is a place for telling once learners have sufficiently differentiated knowledge to use it generatively.
Teachable idea: telling is a mode with a *time*. The parent may tell — a rule, a recipe, an explanation — after the child has met the cases the telling organises, and never before. In practice: no recipe on a family until the child has at least two distinct failed approaches in its record to hang it on.

**Bonawitz, Shafto, Gweon, Goodman, Spelke & Schulz 2011, "The double-edged sword of pedagogy: Instruction limits spontaneous exploration and discovery", Cognition 120(3):322–330 (DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2010.10.001) [V].**
What it says: preschoolers shown one function of a toy by a pedagogical adult explored almost only that function; children shown by a naive adult, or with an interrupted demonstration, explored broadly. The children reason that a teacher who knew of other functions would have shown them, so pedagogy is efficient but costs discovery.
Teachable idea: a demonstration says more than it shows — it implies "this is all there is". So a parent's worked pattern carries an explicit disclaimer the child can repeat ("one of several ways; others exist that I have not shown"), is followed by a problem where that pattern fails, and is never the only pattern shown for a family (Rittle-Johnson & Star's pair).

### E. Socratic questioning and dialogic teaching

**Paul & Elder 2006, The Thinker's Guide to Socratic Questioning, Foundation for Critical Thinking (ISBN 9780944583449) [V record, Open Library]; Foundation for Critical Thinking, "Socratic Teaching" page [V, read].**
What they say: the page states that Socratic teaching focuses on "giving students questions, not answers" and models an inquiring mind by continually probing the subject with questions; the abilities gained from working the elements of reasoning in a disciplined, self-assessing way prepare one for it. Practitioner literature; no data.
Teachable idea: keep questions as the *default* mode for thinking moves — it is the cheapest, least leaky and least copyable — while noting that the tradition itself pairs the questions with explicit teaching of the elements of reasoning. Question-only without that grounding is the caricature, not the method.

**Alexander 2018, "Developing dialogic teaching: genesis, process, trial", Research Papers in Education 33(5):561–598 (DOI 10.1080/02671522.2018.1481140) [V; upgraded from the survey's "recalled"].**
What it says: dialogic teaching framed "not as a single, circumscribed 'method' but as an interlocking set of permissive repertoires" steered by principles of procedure; a UK EEF randomised trial with nearly 5,000 Year 5 pupils and 208 teachers found the intervention group about two months ahead in English, mathematics and science after 20 weeks, with coded video showing the intended changes in teacher and student talk.
Teachable idea: the strongest field evidence for talk-based teaching is for a *repertoire*, explicitly not a single method. A parent's room should have a written repertoire (question forms, suggestion forms, demonstration forms, story forms) and the audit should show all of them used; a parent using one form is, by this trial's own framing, not doing dialogic teaching.

**Mercer, Wegerif & Dawes 1999, "Children's Talk and the Development of Reasoning in the Classroom", British Educational Research Journal 25(1):95–111 (DOI 10.1080/0141192990250107) [V].**
What it says: sixty 9–10-year-olds taught explicit "ground rules" for reasoning talk shifted their language as intended, solved Raven's matrices problems better as groups, and improved individual Raven's scores against matched controls — "the value of explicitly teaching children how to use language to reason".
Teachable idea: the ground rules were *taught explicitly*, not discovered by questioning. The move vocabulary (decide, associate, goal, build, prune, perceive) is such a set of ground rules; it is stated plainly once in the birth brief and in the preparation phase, and the questions come after.

**Chi, Siler, Jeong, Yamauchi & Hausmann 2001, "Learning from human tutoring", Cognitive Science 25(4):471–533 (DOI 10.1207/s15516709cog2504_1) [V].**
What it says: tutors suppressed from giving explanations and feedback, and encouraged only to prompt, produced the same learning as unrestricted tutors; the gain came from deeper and more scaffolding episodes and from students taking more control (reading more), limited by their reading ability.
Teachable idea: this is the strongest single warrant for questions as the default — prompting matched explaining. Read carefully, it says questions are *as good as*, not *better than*, telling, and that the students compensated by reading more. Our child has no textbook to read; the record and the gym are its text, so a prompt must point at a place in the record where the answer can be worked out.

**Chi & Wylie 2014, "The ICAP Framework: Linking Cognitive Engagement to Active Learning Outcomes", Educational Psychologist 49(4):219–243 (DOI 10.1080/00461520.2014.965823) [V].**
What it says: engagement behaviours fall into passive, active, constructive and interactive modes, and learning rises along that order; the framework is validated on note-taking, concept mapping and self-explaining.
Teachable idea: judge a parent mode by what the child *does* with it, not by the mode's name. A worked pattern the child re-derives on a changed problem is constructive; a question the child answers by echo is passive. The instrument is the child's output category, per brief.

### F. Variability and interleaving

**Schmidt & Bjork 1992, "New Conceptualizations of Practice: Common Principles in Three Paradigms Suggest New Concepts for Training", Psychological Science 3(4):207–218 (DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.1992.tb00029.x) [V].**
What it says: across motor and verbal learning, manipulations that maximise performance during training (blocking, constant conditions, frequent feedback) can harm long-term retention and transfer, and manipulations that slow acquisition (variability, spacing, reduced feedback) can help them.
Teachable idea: a parent that makes the child look good this window is probably teaching it less. Varying the *mode* of help is one more variable condition of practice: the same lesson delivered as a question one wake, a story from the record the next, a contrasting pair the third, should depress echo and raise parent-free retention — a testable prediction, not a finding.

**Shea & Morgan 1979, "Contextual interference effects on the acquisition, retention, and transfer of a motor skill", Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory 5(2):179–187 (DOI 10.1037/0278-7393.5.2.179) [V].**
What it says: three motor tasks learned in blocked or random order; random (high interference) acquisition gave better retention under changed conditions and better transfer, most of all on the most complex transfer task.
Teachable idea: interleave the families the child practises *and* the modes the parent uses; the gain shows only under changed conditions — the disjoint panel and the parent-absent window — never on the training panel.

**Rohrer & Taylor 2007, "The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning", Instructional Science 35(6):481–498 (DOI 10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8) [V, Springer abstract]; Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung 2020, "A randomized controlled trial of interleaved mathematics practice", Journal of Educational Psychology 112(1):40–52 (DOI 10.1037/edu0000367) [V].**
What they say: 2007 — spaced practice beat massed and mixed practice beat blocked at a one-week test. 2020 — preregistered cluster trial, 54 seventh-grade classes over four months; interleaved practice beat blocked on an unannounced test one month later, 61% versus 38%, d = 0.83, with teachers needing no training.
Teachable idea: once the child can execute each approach, no two consecutive problems in a window call for the same first move (already the survey's V7 rule); the same logic applied to parenting means no two consecutive briefs on one lesson use the same mode.

**Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler 2008, "Spacing Effects in Learning", Psychological Science 19(11):1095–1102 (DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x) [V].**
What it says: over 1,350 people, a review after a gap of up to 3.5 months and a test up to a year later; the optimal gap grows with the test delay, from about 20–40% of a one-week delay to 5–10% of a one-year delay.
Teachable idea: a lesson, in whatever mode, is returned to at growing intervals (the survey's +1, +2, +4, +8 sleeps) and each return uses a *different* mode from the last — the spacing decides *when*, the rotation decides *how*.

### G. The expert tutor's repertoire

**Lepper & Woolverton 2002, "The Wisdom of Practice: Lessons Learned from the Study of Highly Effective Tutors", in Aronson (ed.), Improving Academic Achievement, Academic Press, pp. 135–158 (DOI 10.1016/B978-012064455-1/50010-5) [V record, Crossref; the INSPIRE summary — intelligent, nurturant, Socratic, progressive, indirect, reflective, encouraging — is recalled, not read here].**
What it says (recalled): expert tutors rarely tell; they are indirect (hints, questions, marking an error's location rather than correcting it), progressive (harder problems as the learner succeeds), and attribute difficulty to the task rather than the learner.
Teachable idea: "indirect" is not "question-only". The expert's indirectness includes suggestions phrased as options, hypotheticals ("suppose the count rose here — then what?"), and pointing at a place in the record; the repertoire is the point.

**Graesser, Person & Magliano 1995, "Collaborative dialogue patterns in naturalistic one-to-one tutoring", Applied Cognitive Psychology 9(6):495–522 (DOI 10.1002/acp.2350090604) [V].**
What it says: unskilled tutors (graduate students; high-schoolers tutoring seventh-graders) showed little of the sophisticated pedagogy of theory or intelligent tutoring systems; the prominent components were collaborative problem solving, question answering and explanation in the context of specific examples.
Teachable idea: a strong model prompted to "tutor" will, like these untrained tutors, default to collaborative solving and explaining on the example in front of it. The room's verifier exists because this default is what a language model does when unwatched.

**Roscoe & Chi 2007, "Understanding Tutor Learning: Knowledge-Building and Knowledge-Telling in Peer Tutors' Explanations and Questions", Review of Educational Research 77(4):534–574 (DOI 10.3102/0034654307309920) [V].**
What it says: peer tutors show a pervasive *knowledge-telling bias* — even when trained, they focus on delivering knowledge rather than developing it — which is why tutor learning gains are often underwhelming.
Teachable idea: the parent's own drift is toward telling. Audit each parent's mode share per window (share of lines that are statements, suggestions, demonstrations, questions, stories); a parent whose telling share climbs for two windows is re-prompted or rotated, exactly as the child's echo is answered by changing the problem.

**VanLehn 2011, "The Relative Effectiveness of Human Tutoring, Intelligent Tutoring Systems, and Other Tutoring Systems", Educational Psychologist 46(4):197–221 (DOI 10.1080/00461520.2011.611369) [V].**
What it says: human tutoring's effect is d = 0.79, not the believed 2.0; step-based intelligent tutors reach 0.76, nearly as much; finer interaction granularity below the step does not add.
Teachable idea: the value of a parent is in reacting at the *step* — one brief on one incident — not in fine-grained running commentary. It also caps expectations: a well-run room is worth fractions of a noise band per stage here, not a transformation.

**Koedinger & Aleven 2007, "Exploring the Assistance Dilemma in Experiments with Cognitive Tutors", Educational Psychology Review 19(3):239–264 (DOI 10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0) [V, Springer abstract].**
What it says: experiments with Cognitive Tutors on feedback, error messages and on-demand hints reinterpreted as partial answers to one question — how a learning environment should balance giving and withholding information; "how best to achieve this balance remains a fundamental open problem".
Teachable idea: name the parent's central problem correctly — the *assistance dilemma* — and treat every mode choice as a data point in it: log what was given or withheld and read the parent-free outcome two sleeps later. Nobody has a closed-form answer; we can have a ledger.

### H. The risk of a single mode: dependence, and what LLM tutors do by default

**Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman 2025, "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics", PNAS 122(26) (DOI 10.1073/pnas.2422633122; a correction was published in PNAS 122(34), DOI 10.1073/pnas.2518204122, not read here) [V].**
What it says: a field experiment with nearly a thousand students; a ChatGPT-like tutor raised practice grades 48% and a guard-railed tutor 127%, but when access was removed the unguarded group scored 17% *worse* than students who never had it; the guardrails largely removed the harm. Students used the answer-giving tool as a "crutch".
Teachable idea: the harm of answer-giving is invisible while the helper is present. Every mode's effect is read in the parent-absent window; and the dependence index — score with the brief in context minus score without it — is a per-mode instrument. A demonstration or recipe whose dependence index does not shrink over successive windows is being leaned on, not learned.

**Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne & Ponti 2025, "AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning", Scientific Reports 15 (DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6) [V, preprint abstract].**
What it says: a randomised study in a college physics course; students learned more than twice as much in less time with an AI tutor built on the same pedagogical practices as the active-learning class, and reported more engagement.
Teachable idea: read beside Bastani, the difference between help and harm was the *design* of the helper, not the presence of a model; the tutor here was scripted with best practices (scaffolding, small steps, checks). The lesson for the room is that the parent's pedagogy is a written artefact to be tested, not an emergent property of a stronger model.

**Macina, Daheim, Chowdhury, Sinha, Kapur, Gurevych & Sachan 2023, "MathDial", Findings of EMNLP 2023, pp. 5602–5621 (DOI 10.18653/v1/2023.findings-emnlp.372; arXiv 2305.14536) [V, abstract via Hugging Face mirror; Crossref record].**
What it says: GPT-3 "fail[s] at tutoring because they generate factually incorrect feedback or are prone to revealing solutions to students too early"; human teachers guided a simulated student with scaffolding questions from a taxonomy of teacher moves; fine-tuning on these dialogues made models better tutors, measured interactively as the trade-off between student success and telling.
Teachable idea: the parent's quality metric is a trade-off curve, not a rule — student success against telling. A parent that never tells sits at one end of the curve; the ruling asks for a parent that moves along it deliberately.

**Kargupta, Agarwal, Tur & Han 2024, "Instruct, Not Assist" (TreeInstruct), Findings of EMNLP 2024, pp. 9475–9495 (DOI 10.18653/v1/2024.findings-emnlp.553; arXiv 2406.11709) [V, abstract via Hugging Face mirror; Crossref record].**
What it says: "current LLMs often give away solutions directly, making them ineffective instructors"; an instructor agent that estimates the student's knowledge state and plans a tree of probing questions guided students to fix multi-bug code with few turns.
Teachable idea: the planning object is the *child's estimated state*, not the question list. A parent that keeps a per-family estimate of what the child can do ("frontier estimate", already logged) and chooses mode from it is doing what this system does.

**Wang, Ribeiro, Robinson, Loeb & Demszky 2024, "Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise", arXiv 2410.03017 [V, arXiv abstract read].**
What it says: a preregistered randomised trial with 900 tutors and 1,800 K-12 students; students of tutors given a model of expert thinking were 4 percentage points more likely to master topics (9 points for lower-rated tutors); tutors with the tool used more guiding questions and were less likely to give away the answer.
Teachable idea: the "second parent" that runs the verifier is this design — a model of expert tutoring watching a tutor. Its effect was largest on the weakest tutors, which is a reason to keep it on every room, not only the ones that look bad.

### I. Grain-of-salt absorption: the child's side of varied modes

**Koenig & Harris 2005, "Preschoolers Mistrust Ignorant and Inaccurate Speakers", Child Development 76(6):1261–1277 (DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8624.2005.00849.x) [V].**
What it says: three experiments (N = 119); four-year-olds tracked which informant had been accurate, predicted future accuracy, and sought and endorsed information from the accurate informant over the inaccurate one; both ages trusted knowledgeable over ignorant speakers.
Teachable idea: a child can weigh a parent's offering only if the parent's *track record* is visible to it. The ledger already holds it; the parent should make it usable — "I suggested pass order P on family F three sleeps ago; check what happened before you take this one" — and the measured disposition is uptake conditional on the advice's record.

**Sperber, Clément, Heintz, Mascaro, Mercier, Origgi & Wilson 2010, "Epistemic Vigilance", Mind & Language 25(4):359–393 (DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0017.2010.01394.x) [V].**
What it says: because humans depend on communication and can be misinformed, they have a suite of cognitive mechanisms for epistemic vigilance — toward the source (competence, benevolence) and toward the content (coherence with what one believes) — which is what keeps communication advantageous.
Teachable idea: vigilance has two checks, source and content. The child can be taught both as moves: "does this parent's past advice on this family pay off?" and "does this recipe fit what my own record says?" A recipe offered by a parent and rejected by the child with a stated reason from its record is a *success* of parenting, not a failure of uptake; count it as such.

## 2. Synthesis: what mixture and sequencing the evidence favours

1. **Mode follows the child's state on *this* thing, not a teaching philosophy.** The expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga 2003, 2007), the fading of worked examples (Renkl & Atkinson 2003), the contingent shift (Wood & Middleton 1975) and Kirschner et al.'s own caveat that guidance recedes with prior knowledge all describe one curve: heavy structure for a novice, questions for the competent, silence for the owner. The unit of "novice" is per family and per move, so one child is a novice and an expert at the same time in different rooms.

2. **Two orderings, keyed by content type.** For domain-general habits — the values and the six moves — explicit statement first and probe second (Alfieri 2011; Sinha & Kapur 2021's reversal; Mercer et al. 1999's explicit ground rules). For gym content — which approach works on which family — attempt first, then instruction built on the child's own attempts and contrasting cases (Kapur 2008, 2014; Loibl et al. 2017; Schwartz & Bransford 1998). The parent should be able to say, of every brief, which ordering it is following and why.

3. **Questions are the default, not the whole.** Prompting matched explaining in Chi et al. 2001, which makes it the safest default: least leaky, least copyable, cheapest to fade. But the same study's learners compensated by reading more, and the dialogic and Socratic traditions themselves pair questions with explicit teaching (Alexander 2018; Mercer 1999; the Foundation's "elements of reasoning"). Question-only for a child that cannot yet answer is Kirschner's minimal guidance.

4. **Demonstration is legitimate and dangerous in a known way.** It is one of Wood, Bruner & Ross's six functions and Collins et al.'s first method; it buys speed on the same structure only (Sweller & Cooper 1985) and it narrows exploration (Bonawitz 2011). So: shown once per new family or move, on an unscored problem, in pairs (Rittle-Johnson & Star 2007), with the disclaimer the child can repeat, and followed by a problem where it fails.

5. **A recipe may be offered when there is a time for telling** — after the child has two distinct failed approaches on that family to hang it on (Schwartz & Bransford 1998; Loibl 2017) — marked as opinion, never repeated verbatim, never alone (Rohin's ruling), and its dependence index (Bastani 2025) read at the next parent-free window.

6. **Vary the mode as you vary the practice.** Variability, spacing and interleaving lower training performance and raise retention and transfer (Schmidt & Bjork 1992; Shea & Morgan 1979; Rohrer 2007, 2020; Cepeda 2008). Mode rotation is the parenting analogue and is a hypothesis to test: no two consecutive briefs on one lesson in the same mode; each spaced return to a lesson in a new mode.

7. **The parent drifts toward telling; audit it.** Untrained tutors collaborate and explain (Graesser 1995); trained peer tutors still knowledge-tell (Roscoe & Chi 2007); language models give the answer away (MathDial; TreeInstruct; Tutor CoPilot). The hedge is a per-parent mode-share audit with a ceiling on any one mode over a window, and the second parent's verifier (Tutor CoPilot's effect was largest on the weakest tutors).

8. **Every mode is judged in the parent's absence.** The harm of a crutch appears only when it is removed (Bastani 2025); the gains of variability appear only under changed conditions (Shea & Morgan 1979). The survey's two-horizon, disjoint-panel, parent-free readout stays the only success signal, now split by mode.

9. **The child is taught to weigh, not filter.** Selective trust and epistemic vigilance (Koenig & Harris 2005; Sperber et al. 2010) say a learner tracks the source's record and the content's fit. A child that rejects a parent's recipe with a reason from its own ledger, and then tests its own line, is the goal state; uptake conditional on the parent's track record is the instrument.

10. **Expect small effects.** Human tutoring is d ≈ 0.8 (VanLehn 2011), the best trials of dialogic teaching are two months' progress in twenty weeks (Alexander 2018); here that is fractions of a noise band per stage. Nothing in this lens promises more.

### A life-course schedule for modes (a proposal for the canon, not a rule yet)

- **Stage 0, preparation.** Explicit statement of the values and the six moves (once, in the birth brief and the opening `[PARENT]` line); one think-aloud demonstration of the format and of each move on an unscored easy problem, in pairs where a contrast exists; then question per move; then silence as each is produced unprompted in ≥ 3 of the last 8 problems. Structuring modes dominate; fade is by move.
- **Each new family in the classroom.** Rounds 1–2: no brief on content; count distinct approaches. Round 3: a brief that cites the child's attempts, shows a contrasting pair from its own record (its approach beside one alternative), and may offer one recipe as opinion if two failed approaches exist. Rounds 4+: questions at the frontier; the contingent shift up or down one rung per outcome; interleaved with other families.
- **Mid-life.** Mode chosen from the frontier estimate and the mode-share audit: the mode used least recently on this lesson that the child's state permits; stories from the record ("on episode 412 you …") as the neutral mode between structuring and problematizing; never the same mode twice running on one lesson; a recipe's dependence index read before it is ever mentioned again.
- **Late life.** Mostly silence and problem selection; the parent speaks in the child's own past words; any recipe spoken is one the child itself wrote; demonstrations only for a genuinely new family, where the cycle restarts.
- **Throughout.** Private time untouched (V13); the leak scan as the only hard shell; the verifier flags over-scaffolding (a mode heavier than the child's state) as readily as leaks.

### Instruments this lens adds (all read beside the score, never a gate, never shown)

Mode share per parent per window (statement / suggestion / demonstration / offered recipe / question / story), with a ceiling flag; uptake by mode (does the child's next-window behaviour change more after one mode than another, on matched lessons); echo by mode (demonstrations and recipes will echo most — that is the cost to price); dependence index by mode (with-brief minus parent-free score, and its trend); parent-free retention by mode at +2 sleeps; contingency audit (share of rung shifts that followed the last outcome in the right direction, Wood & Middleton); grain-of-salt readings — uptake conditional on the parent's track record on that family, disagreement rate, and share of disagreements followed by a test within the window.

## 3. Where this lens meets the existing rulebook (for Rohin's call)

- **Rule 20 ("question, not answer, for every move") and rule 24 ("never transmit a recipe, including a thinking recipe")** are the "nevers" the ruling removes. Proposed replacement: question is the *default* mode for moves; demonstration of a move (think-aloud on an unscored problem) is allowed once per move per stage; an ordered checklist of moves may be *offered* as one way of working, marked as opinion, and is flagged only if repeated verbatim, or if the child reproduces its order across problems without variation (that is the six-pass signature one level up).
- **Rule 11's "a worked pattern may show a thinking move only, in Stages 0–1, once"** widens to: a worked pattern may show a gym move too, on an unscored problem, once per new family, in a pair, with the disclaimer — and must then vanish from context after one wake as now.
- **Rule 4 ("attempt first, brief second; at least half the lines cite the child's attempts")** is the productive-failure and Loibl condition and stays as written; it is what makes an offered recipe a "time for telling" rather than a transmission.
- **Rule 26's fade and 3.2 rule 1's script test** already read the fade and the lock-in; they gain the per-mode dependence index and the mode-share audit as inputs.
- **Open tension (1) from the V14 fold** — demonstration allowed for values but not moves — is resolved by the ruling in favour of demonstration for both, under the fade.
- **Nothing here touches the leak scan** (answers to the scored panel, scores themselves), the score-blindness of room parents, or the Montessori-observer rule for private time.

## Summary (10 lines)

1. The evidence favours no single teaching mode; it favours a ladder — show or state early, ask in the middle, withdraw at the end — whose hinge moves per family and per move by what the child now does unprompted (Kalyuga 2003/2007; Renkl & Atkinson 2003; Wood & Middleton 1975).
2. For domain-general habits (values, thinking moves) state explicitly first, then probe; for gym content, let the child attempt first, then teach from its own attempts and contrasting cases (Alfieri 2011; Sinha & Kapur 2021; Kapur 2008/2014; Loibl 2017; Schwartz & Bransford 1998).
3. Questions are the right default — prompting matched explaining (Chi et al. 2001) — but question-only for a child that cannot yet answer is minimal guidance, the arm that loses (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006).
4. Demonstration is a legitimate scaffolding function (Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976; Collins, Brown & Newman 1987) that buys speed on the same structure only and narrows exploration (Sweller & Cooper 1985; Bonawitz 2011): show once, in pairs, with a disclaimer, then a problem where it fails.
5. A recipe may be offered at its "time for telling" — after two distinct failed approaches exist to hang it on — as opinion, never verbatim twice, never alone, with its dependence index read at the next parent-free window (Bastani 2025: crutch users scored 17% worse once the helper was removed).
6. Varying conditions of practice costs immediate performance and buys retention and transfer (Schmidt & Bjork 1992; Shea & Morgan 1979; Rohrer 2007/2020); mode rotation — no two consecutive briefs on one lesson in the same mode — is the parenting analogue, offered here as a hypothesis to test.
7. Tutors drift toward telling: untrained ones explain (Graesser 1995), trained peers knowledge-tell (Roscoe & Chi 2007), language models give answers away (MathDial 2023; TreeInstruct 2024); a per-parent mode-share audit with a ceiling, plus the second-parent verifier (Tutor CoPilot 2024), is the hedge.
8. Every mode is judged only in the parent's absence, on a changed gym, two sleeps later — the survey's readout, now split by mode.
9. Grain-of-salt absorption is the child's taught disposition, not a harness filter: it tracks the parent's record and the recipe's fit with its own ledger (Koenig & Harris 2005; Sperber et al. 2010); a reasoned rejection followed by the child's own test counts as success.
10. Rules 20 and 24 become defaults with allowances, not nevers; rule 11's single worked pattern widens to once per new family, in pairs; the leak scan stays the only hard shell; expected gains are fractions of a noise band per stage (VanLehn 2011: human tutoring d = 0.79).
