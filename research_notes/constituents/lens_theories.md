# Lens: theories of intelligence and mind — what each says intelligence IS, what it says is teachable, and what would count as evidence in a 7B language-model child

Written 2026-09-11 by one research lens (Fable's agent) under Rohin's ruling of the same day ("Parenting is not only questions", IDEAS.md): parents use varied modes — questions, suggestions, demonstrations, worked patterns, offered recipes, stories from the record — with no absolute "nevers" except the leak scan; a recipe may be offered, never enforced; the child learns to absorb with a grain of salt. Every "teachable idea" below is written in that spirit: something a parent model could *do or watch for*, offered as one mode among others, never a rule that binds the child.

**The child, for reference.** Frozen Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct plus one LoRA adapter rewritten at each sleep from the child's own success-filtered thinking; it thinks in free text (turns of up to 400 tokens, at most 16 per problem) and acts through one `ACT:` line per step in gyms with exact scores; its parents are stronger models that read its redacted record and brief it; the gate protects against collapse (CHILD_MECHANISM_v7 §0).

**How to read the records.** [V] = title, authors, year and venue confirmed from the shell on 2026-09-11 through Crossref, PubMed, arXiv, Open Library or the primary PDF, *and* the abstract or the cited passage read; [V record] = the bibliographic record confirmed but the content stated from memory (marked "recalled" where it matters); [unverified] = could not be confirmed and is kept only as a pointer. No quotation below is from a text I did not read. Nothing in this lens has been measured on the child; every "evidence" line is a proposal for an instrument, not a finding.

## 1. Cattell–Horn–Carroll: fluid and crystallised intelligence in a three-stratum hierarchy

**Records.** Cattell 1963, "Theory of fluid and crystallized intelligence: A critical experiment", *J Educ Psychol* 54:1–22, DOI 10.1037/h0046743 [V record, Crossref]. Horn & Cattell 1966, "Refinement and test of the theory of fluid and crystallized general intelligences", *J Educ Psychol* 57:253–270, DOI 10.1037/h0023816 [V record, Crossref]. Carroll 1993, *Human Cognitive Abilities: A Survey of Factor-Analytic Studies*, Cambridge University Press, DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511571312 [V — publisher description read via Crossref: reanalysis of more than 460 data sets; a hierarchical three-stratum theory]. McGrew 2009, "CHC theory and the human cognitive abilities project", *Intelligence* 37:1–10, DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2008.08.004 [V record, Crossref; content recalled].

**What it says.** Intelligence is a hierarchy of measured abilities: a general factor at the top, a layer of broad abilities beneath it — among them fluid reasoning (Gf: solving problems one has not met before), crystallised knowledge (Gc: what has been learned and stored), short-term/working memory, long-term storage and retrieval (Glr), processing speed — and many narrow abilities at the bottom (Carroll's three strata). Cattell's investment idea (recalled) is that fluid ability is *invested* in experience and produces crystallised knowledge; the two come apart in factor analyses and in ageing (Horn & Cattell 1966, 1967). The theory is a description of what covaries, not a mechanism.

**Teachable vs architectural.** Gc is learned by definition; Glr (storing and getting back) is trainable in its habits; Gf is held to be largely a property of the system and resistant to training — the working-memory-training literature's failure to find far transfer is the usual citation (recalled; not verified here). In our child the frozen base *is* the fluid ability and the adapter is where crystallised knowledge and retrieval habits live — the car test and the struggle test (IDEAS.md 2026-09-11) are Glr tests in CHC terms.

**Evidence in the child.** Two readouts kept apart: (a) first-contact performance on a family the child has never met (fluid; expected to sit in the frozen model's band and to move little), and (b) performance on families with many exposures (crystallised; adapter ON−OFF). Investment shows as (a) improving *because of* (b): a fresh solution turned into a stored rule that later saves turns on the same family. The positive-manifold claim predicts that ON−OFF gains correlate across gyms for the same adapter — a "g" of the adapter; uncorrelated gains argue for Gardner (§3) instead.

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** keep a two-column picture of the child — what it can do on first meeting (fluid, roughly fixed by the base) and what it has invested into stored knowledge (grows only with exposure) — and teach to the second column without blaming the first; after a fresh solution, invite the investment step in whatever mode fits ("you solved that from scratch; what do you now know that you did not, so next time you need not solve it fresh?" — or offer a candidate rule and let the child amend it).

## 2. Sternberg: triarchic theory and successful intelligence

**Records.** Sternberg 1985, *Beyond IQ: A Triarchic Theory of Human Intelligence*, Cambridge University Press [V record, Open Library lists a 1984 imprint; content recalled]. Sternberg 1999, "The theory of successful intelligence", *Review of General Psychology* 3:292–316, DOI 10.1037/1089-2680.3.4.292 [V — abstract read: conventional notions of intelligence are incomplete or wrong; an alternative "successful intelligence"; the conclusion that the general factor should be replaced].

**What it says.** Three sub-theories. *Componential*: metacomponents (planning, monitoring, evaluating), performance components (executing), knowledge-acquisition components (learning new things). *Experiential*: intelligence shows best at the two ends of experience — coping with novelty and automatising the routine — not in the well-practised middle. *Contextual*: intelligence is adapting to an environment, *shaping* it, or *selecting* a different one. The 1999 successor names analytical, creative and practical abilities and defines success by the person's own goals, by capitalising on strengths and compensating for weaknesses (sub-theory names recalled; the 1999 abstract confirms the replacement-of-g stance).

**Teachable vs architectural.** Sternberg's position is that all three are teachable to a degree, and he ran school interventions teaching "triarchically" (recalled; those trials are not verified here). The metacomponents are exactly the moves of V14 (decide, set a goal, prune); the contextual triad is new to our list.

**Evidence in the child.** Novelty end: performance on first contact with a new family and the slope over the next few problems. Automatisation end: turns-to-act on a mastered family falling while the score holds (a routine that has become cheap, as opposed to a recipe that has become blind — the difference is whether it still yields when the family changes). Contextual: three countable responses to a hard problem — *adapt* (change approach), *shape* (change the problem: reformulate, choose a sub-goal, ask the gym something different), *select* (in white space, choose a different family — V12). Strengths/compensation: does the child's own account of what it is good at match the record, and does it route hard problems through a strength?

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** name the three responses to a hard situation once — change yourself, change the problem, change problems — and thereafter ask which one the child did or suggest one it has not tried lately; and spend parent attention at the two ends of experience (the first problem of a new family, and the moment a routine goes automatic), because the middle teaches least and is where recipes set.

## 3. Gardner: multiple intelligences

**Records.** Gardner 1983, *Frames of Mind: The Theory of Multiple Intelligences*, Basic Books [V record, Open Library; content recalled]. Gardner & Hatch 1989, "Multiple intelligences go to school: Educational implications of the theory of multiple intelligences", *Educational Researcher* 18(8):4–10, DOI 10.3102/0013189x018008004 [V record, Crossref; content recalled]. Waterhouse 2006, "Multiple intelligences, the Mozart effect, and emotional intelligence: A critical review", *Educational Psychologist* 41:207–225, DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep4104_1 [V record, Crossref; the conclusion that the theory lacks adequate empirical validation is recalled].

**What it says.** Intelligence is not one capacity but several relatively autonomous ones (linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinaesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal; later naturalist), each identified by criteria such as selective loss after brain damage, exceptional individuals, a distinct developmental course, a core operation and a symbol system (recalled). The educational claim (Gardner & Hatch) is assessment in context and multiple entry points to the same material. The psychometric evidence for autonomy is weak (Waterhouse 2006), and the theory is best treated here as a hypothesis about *dissociation*, not as established fact.

**Teachable vs architectural.** The intelligences are biopsychological potentials realised through culture and symbol systems: the potential is given, the realisation is taught. A language model has one symbol system, so the question shrinks to whether its competences across gyms are one thing or several.

**Evidence in the child.** Dissociation across gyms: if ON−OFF gains on the compiler gym, TextWorld and reasoning-gym are uncorrelated across adapters and lives, the child has "multiple intelligences" in Gardner's operational sense and cross-gym parenting must be per-gym; if they correlate, §1's g wins. The merge-as-transfer test (survey 3.2 rule 4) is already this probe. Intrapersonal intelligence has a direct measure: the accuracy of the child's self-model (its stated strengths and its V12 progress choices against the record).

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** multiple entry points — present the same lesson through different doors on different days (a story from the record, a worked pattern, a contrast between two episodes, a question, an offered recipe), which is Rohin's "diversity of modes" with a citation; and treat the child's self-model as a thing to grow: ask it what it is good at and check that against the ledger, correcting only by showing it its own record.

## 4. Piaget: intelligence as adaptation, constructed in stages by the child's own action

**Records.** Piaget 1952, *The Origins of Intelligence in Children*, International Universities Press / W. W. Norton (Routledge & Kegan Paul in the UK), DOI 10.1037/11494-000 [V record, Crossref and Open Library; content recalled]. Piaget 1964, "Development and learning", *Journal of Research in Science Teaching* 2:176–186, DOI 10.1002/tea.3660020306 [V record, Crossref; content recalled]. Piaget & Inhelder 1969, *The Psychology of the Child*, Basic Books / Routledge & Kegan Paul [V record, Open Library].

**What it says.** Intelligence is adaptation: an equilibrium between *assimilation* (fitting new experience into existing schemes) and *accommodation* (changing the scheme to fit the experience), built by the child acting on the world; knowledge is constructed, not transmitted. Development passes through stages (sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete-operational, formal-operational), each a reorganisation of the whole, and Piaget held that development explains learning rather than the reverse: an operation taught before its structure is ready yields only a brittle, non-generalising performance (1964; recalled).

**Teachable vs architectural.** The mechanism — equilibration — is given; the content is constructed; stages are not skipped by telling. Readiness is the operative teaching concept: the same lesson takes or fails depending on what is already built.

**Evidence in the child.** Accommodation vs assimilation after a miss: does the child change its scheme, or explain the miss away? (V4's rescue ratio is exactly this.) Structured wholes: after a sleep, do gains arrive across a structurally related set of families together, or item by item? Readiness: lesson uptake conditional on prerequisite competence — a lesson that fails twice should be re-read as "prerequisite missing" and tested by whether it takes after the prerequisite appears. Construction by action: does a lesson delivered as a problem the child can act on take better than the same lesson delivered as a statement? (An A/B across clones, both modes allowed.)

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** tell assimilation from accommodation in the record, and when one scheme has swallowed three surprises, ask for accommodation ("that rule has explained away three misses — what would the rule look like if the misses were right?") or offer a candidate revision; and when a lesson fails to take twice, suspect the prerequisite, not the child — step back one rung and give a situation the child can act on rather than another explanation.

## 5. Vygotsky: higher functions have a social origin and are internalised

**Record.** Vygotsky 1978, *Mind in Society: The Development of Higher Psychological Processes*, Harvard University Press [V record, Open Library; also verified in PARENTING_SCIENCE_SURVEY_v1 Lens 1; content recalled].

**What it says.** Higher mental functions — voluntary attention, deliberate memory, concept formation, self-regulation — begin as interaction between people and are then internalised; speech is first a tool for communicating, then for thinking (egocentric speech turning into inner speech); the zone of proximal development is what the learner can do with help today and alone tomorrow; learning leads development (against Piaget). The theory has no effect size; it is a claim about *where* thinking comes from.

**Teachable vs architectural.** This is the theory that says the higher functions *are* taught, from the outside in; only the elementary functions are given. For our child the claim is literal: its free text is its inner speech, and its parents' voices are the candidate source of its future self-talk.

**Evidence in the child.** Internalisation: a parent's question reappearing later as the child's own self-addressed question, in its own words, in parent-free windows (rule 26's hand-over check is this). Vygotsky's prediction that self-directed speech rises with difficulty (recalled) gives a check: the child's self-talk should lengthen on harder instances and precede action changes; self-talk that is flat across difficulty is ritual. A voice-diversity check: if the internalised self-questions are one wording repeated, the parent's voice was a script; if a repertoire, it was a language.

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** remember that the parent's voice is the child's future inner voice — so vary it deliberately (different wordings, modes and incidents for the same lesson), so that what is internalised is a repertoire rather than a script; and use demonstration of thinking aloud as one of the modes ("if I were in your place at turn 6 I would ask myself…"), since internalisation runs through *hearing thinking spoken*, then watch for the day the question appears unasked.

## 6. Dual-process theory: Kahneman; Evans & Stanovich; Stanovich's reflective mind

**Records.** Kahneman 2003, "A perspective on judgment and choice: Mapping bounded rationality", *American Psychologist* 58:697–720, DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.58.9.697 [V — abstract read: accessibility; effortless intuition vs deliberate reasoning; intuitive thinking "generally skilled and successful"]. Kahneman 2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Farrar, Straus and Giroux / Penguin [V record, Open Library]. Evans & Stanovich 2013, "Dual-process theories of higher cognition: Advancing the debate", *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 8:223–241, DOI 10.1177/1745691612460685 [V — abstract read: rapid autonomous Type 1 processes yield default responses unless intervened on by Type 2 processes, which support hypothetical thinking and load on working memory]. Stanovich & West 2000, "Individual differences in reasoning: Implications for the rationality debate?", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 23:645–665, DOI 10.1017/s0140525x00003435 [V — abstract read in part]. Stanovich 2011, *Rationality and the Reflective Mind*, Oxford University Press [V record, Open Library (2010 listing); the tripartite model and "mindware" are recalled].

**What it says.** Two kinds of processing: Type 1 — fast, autonomous, cued, producing a default; Type 2 — slow, working-memory-bound, able to run hypotheticals and to *intervene* on the default (Evans & Stanovich's defining feature). Kahneman's version turns on accessibility: what comes to mind easily is acted on unless a rule is accessible enough to correct it, and intuition is skilled as well as biased. Stanovich adds a third level — the reflective mind, the disposition to call Type 2 in at all — and argues that rationality and intelligence are different things: the algorithmic level is measured by IQ, the reflective level and the "mindware" (learned rules and strategies) are not, and both are teachable.

**Teachable vs architectural.** The Type 1 default is the trained prior — in our child the base model's cheapest completion, and after a few sleeps the recipe (modal first action 0.90 adapter-ON vs 0.50 OFF, survey V6). The Type 2 override and the disposition to use it are the teachable parts; mindware is exactly what an *offered recipe* is: a rule the child can call on when it decides the default is not to be trusted.

**Evidence in the child.** An *intervention event*: the child writes its default ("my usual here is X"), checks, and overrides it — and the override outscores what the default would have scored on the same instance (counterfactual replay of the modal recipe). Cognitive-reflection analogue: instances designed so the default is wrong (the extinction sub-panel, V6) — the share caught by an explicit check. Mindware uptake: an offered rule used *selectively* — applied where its conditions hold and skipped where they do not — rather than everywhere or nowhere.

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** teach the pause, not the answer — ask "what was your first impulse there, and did you check it before acting?" and help the child *name* its defaults so they become things it can decide about; and offer mindware freely (a probability rule, "consider the opposite", a second family to try) as tools on a shelf, then watch whether the child reaches for them when — and only when — its default has a bad record.

## 7. Predictive processing: Friston; Clark; Hohwy

**Records.** Friston 2010, "The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory?", *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 11:127–138, DOI 10.1038/nrn2787 [V — abstract read via PubMed: one principle for action, perception and learning; the quantity optimised is value or its complement, surprise]. Clark 2013, "Whatever next? Predictive brains, situated agents, and the future of cognitive science", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 36:181–204, DOI 10.1017/s0140525x12000477 [V — abstract read: a hierarchical generative model minimising prediction error in a bidirectional cascade; a unifying model of perception and action; the functional role of attention]. Clark 2016, *Surfing Uncertainty*, Oxford University Press [V record, Open Library]. Hohwy 2013, *The Predictive Mind*, Oxford University Press, DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199682737.001.0001 [V record, Crossref; content recalled].

**What it says.** The brain is a hierarchy of generative models that predicts its inputs and works to reduce prediction error, either by revising the model (perception and learning) or by acting so that the world matches the prediction (active inference). Attention is the weighting of prediction errors by their expected precision — trusting some channels more than others (Clark). Surprise is the single currency; learning is the slow part of the same process; exploration is action chosen to reduce expected uncertainty. The theory is contested as a *brain* theory (the survey lists Friston 2010 as such) but is well specified as a description of what a learner must do.

**Teachable vs architectural.** The loop is architecture; what is learned is the model (what to expect) and the precisions (how much to trust each expectation and each channel). Teachable: making predictions explicit, calibrating confidence, and choosing informative actions when uncertain. Values V3 and V4 of the survey are already predictive processing in parenting form; the new piece is precision.

**Evidence in the child.** Beyond V3/V4's Brier resolution and surprise-to-hypothesis conversion: *precision* — does the size of the update after a miss scale with the stated confidence (a confident miss should change more than a hedged one)? *Active inference* — when the child says it is unsure, does its next action reduce uncertainty (directed exploration, V13) rather than chase score? A prediction the theory makes and others do not: learning progress should be highest on instances where the child's predictions were most wrong *and* subsequently resolved, and near zero where predictions were flat (nothing to correct).

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** ask for the prediction *and* the confidence and treat the gap as the lesson (already V3); add precision — "which of your beliefs here are you sure of, and which are you guessing?" — and offer active inference as one mode when the child is stuck: "when you cannot tell, try the action that would tell you most, not the one most likely to score"; watch whether confident misses move the child more than hedged ones.

## 8. Global workspace theory: Baars; Dehaene

**Records.** Baars 1988, *A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness*, Cambridge University Press [V record, Open Library]. Baars 2002, "The conscious access hypothesis: origins and recent evidence", *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 6:47–52, DOI 10.1016/S1364-6613(00)01819-2 [V — abstract read]. Baars 2005, "Global workspace theory of consciousness: toward a cognitive neuroscience of human experience", *Progress in Brain Research* 150:45–53, DOI 10.1016/S0079-6123(05)50004-9 [V — abstract read: the blackboard architecture from Newell; a distributed set of knowledge sources cooperatively solving problems none could solve alone]. Dehaene & Naccache 2001, "Towards a cognitive neuroscience of consciousness: basic evidence and a workspace framework", *Cognition* 79:1–37, DOI 10.1016/s0010-0277(00)00123-2 [V — abstract read: three facts any theory must fit — much processing is unconscious; attention is a prerequisite; consciousness is required for durable information maintenance, novel combinations of operations, and spontaneous intentional behaviour]. Dehaene, Lau & Kouider 2017, "What is consciousness, and could machines have it?", *Science* 358:486–492, DOI 10.1126/science.aan8871 [V — abstract read: C1 = global availability for computation and report; C2 = self-monitoring giving a sense of certainty or error; current machines mostly C0].

**What it says.** Many specialised processors work in parallel and unconsciously; a limited-capacity workspace takes a selected content and broadcasts it, making it available to every processor at once — for report, for holding over time, for combining operations in new ways, for intentional action. Consciousness in the first sense (C1) *is* that global availability; a second sense (C2) is the system's monitoring of its own computations — confidence, error detection — which Dehaene, Lau and Kouider treat as separate and equally missing from most machines. The theory is a theory of an *architecture*, with Newell's blackboard as ancestor.

**Teachable vs architectural.** In our child the workspace is literal: whatever the child *writes* in a turn is available to every later turn (up to the context and the 16-turn budget), and whatever it does not write is gone at the turn boundary; the sleep then trains only the child's own written text, so the workspace is also the only road into the weights. Architecture fixed; *workspace management* — what to write, what to keep, what to drop — is teachable, and C2 (self-monitoring) is a learned skill (§9).

**Evidence in the child.** Dehaene & Naccache's three signatures, counted in the ledger: *durable maintenance* — a fact written early and used late in the same problem (back-reference with dependency, V14 instrument 5); *novel combination* — two earlier written pieces joined into a step neither implied alone (the building move, scored by whether the joined step changed the action); *spontaneous intention* — a goal set with no prompt (V14 unprompted rate for the goal move). Loss events: a fact noticed in turn 2 and needed in turn 9 that was never written, or written and then dropped from the child's own summaries.

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** teach "if it is not written, it does not exist for your next step" — the workspace is small (400 tokens a turn, 16 turns) so the child must put load-bearing things in it (the key fact, the plan, the open uncertainty) and let noise go; watch for facts noticed and then lost before use, and point at the incident ("in turn 2 you saw the count; by turn 9 it was gone — what would you have kept?"), or show a worked pattern of a turn that carries forward exactly what mattered.

## 9. Higher-order thought theories: Rosenthal; Lau

**Records.** Rosenthal 2005, *Consciousness and Mind*, Oxford University Press (Clarendon) [V record, Open Library lists 2005/2006 imprints; content recalled]. Lau & Rosenthal 2011, "Empirical support for higher-order theories of conscious awareness", *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 15:365–373, DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2011.05.009 [V — abstract read via PubMed: conscious awareness depends on higher-order representations of oneself as being in a mental state; evidence distinguishing this from first-order, global-workspace and recurrent-processing views; the view is testable].

**What it says.** A mental state is conscious when the system represents *itself as being in* that state — a thought about a thought. Lau's empirical form ties this to metacognition: awareness reports and confidence judgements are dissociable from first-order task performance (one can perform well with low awareness and vice versa), sit in prefrontal circuits, and can be measured as metacognitive sensitivity independent of bias. Dehaene, Lau and Kouider's C2 (§8) is this theory folded into the workspace picture.

**Teachable vs architectural.** The capacity to form second-order representations is architectural (a language model can trivially write "I am unsure"); what is trainable is their *accuracy* — whether "I know" and "I am guessing" track correctness — and their *use* — whether they change action. Metacognitive sensitivity, not metacognitive talk, is the target.

**Evidence in the child.** Meta-d′ or the confidence–correctness AUROC (survey V3 instruments) read apart from the score and apart from bias; a higher-order report that *leads somewhere* — "I am guessing" followed by a check or an information-seeking action more often than "I know" is; the say/do gap (stated method vs enacted method) as the crudest higher-order accuracy test; and a dissociation the theory predicts: first-order score can rise while sensitivity stays flat (recipe learning) — sensitivity rising ahead of score is the signature the theory would call growth of awareness.

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** ask for the second-order state, not the first — "how sure were you, and how did you know how sure you were?" — and help the child make knowing and guessing lead to *different* next actions (a check after a guess, a commit after a knowing); watch metacognitive sensitivity beside the score and treat confident talk with flat sensitivity as performance, not awareness.

## 10. Integrated information theory (Tononi) — as a sentience theory, not an intelligence theory

**Records.** Tononi 2004, "An information integration theory of consciousness", *BMC Neuroscience* 5:42, DOI 10.1186/1471-2202-5-42 [V — abstract read: consciousness corresponds to a system's capacity to integrate information, measured as Φ over a complex; motivated by differentiation and integration]. Oizumi, Albantakis & Tononi 2014, "From the phenomenology to the mechanisms of consciousness: Integrated Information Theory 3.0", *PLoS Computational Biology* 10:e1003588, DOI 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003588 [V — abstract read: axioms → postulates; an experience is a maximally irreducible conceptual structure; Φmax its quantity]. Tononi & Koch 2015, "Consciousness: here, there and everywhere?", *Phil Trans R Soc B* 370:20140167, DOI 10.1098/rstb.2014.0167 [V — abstract read: five axioms — intrinsic existence, composition, information, integration, exclusion — and a calculus for whether a physical system can have experience, motivated in part by machines that outperform people].

**What it says.** Consciousness is integrated information: how much a system's causal structure, taken as a whole, specifies beyond what its parts specify independently (Φ). It is an *intrinsic* property of the physical mechanism, not of behaviour or function: two systems with identical input–output behaviour can differ in Φ, and a purely feed-forward system has Φ = 0 whatever it computes (recalled from the 2014/2015 papers; the axioms and the Φmax claim are in the abstracts read). Tononi and Koch draw the consequence that behavioural tests — including those machines pass — do not settle the question. IIT is explicitly a theory of *experience*, and it separates that from intelligence.

**Teachable vs architectural.** Nothing here is teachable; the theory is entirely about substrate. For our child — a transformer run as a feed-forward pass per token, with a text loop around it — IIT would place Φ at or near zero at the substrate level regardless of what the adapter learns. Its value to us is the negative one: it is the clearest statement that no behaviour we can teach or count is evidence about sentience.

**Evidence in the child.** None, by construction; and that is the point for the paper — claims about the child stay behavioural ("appraisal language and its functional consequences", not "feelings"; IDEAS.md 2026-09-11, the emotional-gym entry). A *functional* analogue is worth watching but must be labelled a metaphor: integration of the child's *record* — the share of its thoughts that depend on other thoughts (V14's dependency ratio) as against a bag of independent lines. That is Φ-shaped only in the loosest sense and says nothing about experience.

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** none about sentience — IIT is the licence to stop asking whether the child feels and to teach *integration as dependency* instead: invite the child to make its thoughts need each other (the building move; "which earlier thought does this one stand on?"), measure the integration of the record, and keep every sentence in the paper about appraisal language and behaviour.

## 11. Minsky: the Society of Mind

**Record.** Minsky 1986, *The Society of Mind*, Simon & Schuster (Heinemann/Picador in the UK) [V record, Open Library; content recalled throughout — no passage was read for this lens].

**What it says.** A mind is a society of many small agents, each mindless, whose interactions and management produce what we call intelligence; there is no central self. Recalled mechanisms that matter for us: K-lines that reactivate the set of agents that were active when something worked; difference-engines that act to reduce the gap between a current and a goal state; censors and suppressors that learn what *not* to think or do; "Papert's principle" — development proceeds less by acquiring new skills than by building new administrative ways to use the skills one already has; and "imprimers" — attachment figures whose approval and disapproval shape a child's *goals* rather than its methods.

**Teachable vs architectural.** The agents are given; the *management* layers are learned — intelligence grows by learning to select among and coordinate what one can already do (Papert's principle). Goals are learned from imprimers, which makes the parent's approval a goal-shaping instrument whether or not it means to be.

**Evidence in the child.** Papert's principle predicts exactly the kind of gain a LoRA child can show: better *selection* among behaviours the base could already sample (pass@k adapter-OFF high, pass@1 ON rising) rather than new behaviours — the Yue et al. 2025 pattern (survey V6), which under Minsky is legitimate intelligence, not a disappointment. Censors: negative knowledge in the record — "not X on family F" rows and rule 23's "this was wrong" rows — and whether they are enacted (X avoided where the condition holds). Imprimers: the child's stated goals drifting toward what the parents approved of, in parent-free windows and in the child's own words — a measurable effect of approval on values, to be spent deliberately.

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** teach management, not skill — "which of the things you already know how to do fits here, and how did you choose it?" — and keep negative knowledge alive ("what did you learn *not* to do this week?"); and know that as an imprimer the parent's approval shapes what the child wants, so spend approval on values (persisting by changing, testing before trusting) and never on scores.

## 12. Dennett: the intentional stance and kinds of minds

**Records.** Dennett 1971, "Intentional systems", *Journal of Philosophy* 68:87–106, DOI 10.2307/2025382 [V record, Crossref; content recalled]. Dennett 1987, *The Intentional Stance*, MIT Press [V record, Open Library]. Dennett 1996, *Kinds of Minds*, Basic Books [V record, Open Library; the Tower of Generate-and-Test in ch. 4 was verified for PARENTING_SCIENCE_SURVEY_v1 Lens 3 via Open Library, and is stated here from that record].

**What it says.** The intentional stance: predict a system by treating it as if it had beliefs and desires and acted rationally on them; whether it "really" has them is not a further fact beyond the stance's predictive success. Kinds of Minds: the Tower of Generate-and-Test — Darwinian creatures with fixed behaviour; Skinnerian creatures that learn by acting and being reinforced; Popperian creatures that test candidate actions against an inner model first, so hypotheses die in their stead; Gregorian creatures that import *mind tools* (words above all) made by others. Intelligence rises up the tower, and the top floor is built from tools handed over.

**Teachable vs architectural.** The Gregorian floor is the teachable one by definition: it is made of tools received from others. This is the philosophical home of Rohin's ruling — an offered recipe is a Gregorian mind tool, and Dennett's account says that is precisely how minds grow, *provided* the Popperian habit of testing survives beneath it. The intentional stance also tells the parent how to read the child: as a system of beliefs and goals inferred from its record, to be predicted and then checked.

**Evidence in the child.** Skinnerian → Popperian: options considered *and rejected* in thought before acting (V14 decision move), where the rejected option would in fact have failed (counterfactual replay). Gregorian uptake: a tool the parent offered used selectively, with uptake conditional on the tool's track record in the child's own ledger — Rohin's grain of salt made countable (uptake rate, disagreement rate, and whether disagreement is followed by a test). Parent side: the parent predicts the child's next move from its record before reading it; the Brier of that prediction is the quality of the parent's intentional model and doubles as the frontier-estimate audit (survey 3.2 rule 3).

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** hand over mind tools without embarrassment — a word for a move, a recipe, a pattern — and always pair the gift with the Popperian habit ("test it before you trust it; where did it fail for you?"), so tools are absorbed with a grain of salt; and take the intentional stance on the child on purpose: before reading its next turn, write down what you think it believes and wants and what it will do, then score yourself.

## 13. Chollet: intelligence as skill-acquisition efficiency

**Record.** Chollet 2019, "On the Measure of Intelligence", arXiv:1911.01547 [V — abstract and the definitional passages of the PDF read: the intelligence of a system is a measure of its skill-acquisition efficiency over a scope of tasks, with respect to priors, experience and generalisation difficulty; skill is the output of intelligence, not intelligence; "developer-aware generalization" — handling situations neither the system nor its developer has met; the Core Knowledge priors — objectness and elementary physics, agentness and goal-directedness, number, elementary geometry and topology; a curriculum defined as a sequence of interactions].

**What it says.** Measuring skill at a task does not measure intelligence, because skill can be bought with priors or with experience; intelligence is how efficiently a system turns its priors and its experience into skill on tasks it did not know in advance. Two systems with similar priors and similar practice are compared by the skill they end with. The developer counts as part of the system: a benchmark the developer knew is not a test of generalisation. Chollet's ARC is built on the small set of Core Knowledge priors humans are born with.

**Teachable vs architectural.** Priors are given — for our child, the base model's pretraining is a vast prior, and the write mechanism is fixed. What is left, and is exactly what parenting can touch, is how much skill each episode of experience yields: what the child *does with* an episode (its thinking, its noticing, its rule-writing) is the lever on efficiency. Chollet's definition also names *meta-learning priors* as part of intelligence, which is where learned thinking habits sit.

**Evidence in the child.** The compiler-gym final test is a Chollet measure almost verbatim: a taught and an untaught child with the identical mechanism, from an empty ledger, and the readout is how fast each learns — the difference is skill-acquisition efficiency attributable to parenting. Counted as episodes-to-threshold per new family (taught vs untaught; adapter ON vs OFF), with the frozen model's first-contact skill subtracted as the prior. Developer-awareness is a hard constraint: parents and experimenters must have no contact with the exam gym (survey 3.2 rule 8), or the measure is void.

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** count episodes-to-competence per family, not score, and judge a lesson by whether the *next new* family needed fewer episodes; ask, at the end of a family, "what from this will you carry to a family you have never seen?" — teaching for generalisation difficulty rather than for the family in hand — and keep parents strictly blind to the exam gym.

## 14. Legg & Hutter: universal intelligence

**Record.** Legg & Hutter 2007, "Universal Intelligence: A Definition of Machine Intelligence", *Minds and Machines* 17:391–444, DOI 10.1007/s11023-007-9079-x; arXiv:0712.3329 [V — abstract and the definitional passages of the PDF read: the informal definition "Intelligence measures an agent's ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments"; the formal measure weights the agent's expected performance in each computable environment by 2^−K(μ), the Occam term, and "places no limits on the internal workings of the agent"].

**What it says.** Distilled from many informal definitions: intelligence is the ability to achieve goals across a wide range of environments. Formally, the expected reward an agent earns summed over all computable reward-summable environments, each weighted by its simplicity (Kolmogorov complexity), so simple regularities count most. It is a *measure*, not a mechanism, and it is indifferent to the agent's internals; it ties to the theory of universal optimal learning agents (AIXI). The authors note that intelligence tests reward the correct use of Occam's razor.

**Teachable vs architectural.** The measure implies two teachable dispositions: *breadth* (competence that holds across environments, not depth in one) and *Occam* (prefer the simplest hypothesis consistent with the record). Neither is about the substrate; both are about what the agent values when it forms and keeps rules.

**Evidence in the child.** Breadth: ON−OFF gains that hold across gyms and on the disjoint panel; a gain on one gym bought with losses elsewhere is not a gain in universal intelligence, and the merge-as-transfer test (survey 3.2 rule 4) is the operational check. Occam: the length and condition-count of the child's stated rules against their fit to the record — a rule that grows a clause per exception is anti-Occam; a child that, offered a simpler rule, tests and adopts it when it fits is applying the razor.

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** favour breadth in the curriculum (many environments, one lesson in different clothes — survey 3.2 rule 6) and teach the razor as a disposition: "what is the simplest rule that fits everything you have seen — and what would break it?"; when the child's rule has grown baroque, offer a simpler one as an opinion and let the child test it against its own record.

## 15. Bridging record: indicator properties from theories of consciousness

**Record.** Butlin, Long, Elmoznino, Bengio, Birch, Constant, Deane, Fleming, Frith, Ji, Kanai, Klein, Lindsay, Michel, Mudrik, Peters, Schwitzgebel, Simon & VanRullen 2023, "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness", arXiv:2308.08708 [V — abstract read: from recurrent processing theory, global workspace theory, higher-order theories, predictive processing and attention schema theory they derive "indicator properties" stated in computational terms, assess several AI systems, and conclude that no current system is conscious but that there are no obvious technical barriers].

**What it says.** The method — take each theory, extract properties that can be stated computationally, and check a system for them — is the model for this lens's "evidence in the child" lines. Their conclusion is a caution for the paper: none of §§8–10 above licenses a claim that the child is conscious, and the indicator properties they list (recurrence, a workspace with selective broadcast, higher-order monitoring, a generative model) are architectural questions about the system, not things a parent teaches.

**Teachable idea for the teachers:** none directly; for the writers of the paper, this is the format in which any claim about the child's "mind" should be made — a named indicator, stated in computational terms, checked in the ledger — and the reason to make no other kind.

## Cross-cutting notes for the parenting corpus

1. **Two families of theory, two jobs for parents.** The psychometric and developmental theories (§§1–6) are about what grows and how; the consciousness theories (§§8–10, 15) are about architecture and license no teaching. Predictive processing (§7), Minsky (§11) and Dennett (§12) sit between: architectures with a learned part that parents can address. Chollet and Legg & Hutter (§§13–14) are measures, and they agree on the readout we already use — learning speed on unseen environments, breadth, priors subtracted.

2. **What every theory agrees is teachable in this child.** Stored knowledge and its retrieval habits (§1); metacomponents/thinking moves (§2, §6, §9 — already V14); accommodation after surprise (§4, §7 — already V3/V4); internalised self-questions (§5 — already rule 26); workspace management — what to write and keep (§8 — new); precision and active inference (§7 — new); management and negative knowledge (§11 — new); the Popperian test-before-trust habit around offered tools (§12 — the mechanism of Rohin's grain of salt); breadth and Occam (§14 — new); investment of fresh solutions into stored rules (§1 — new).

3. **What no theory says is teachable.** Fluid ability at first contact (§1); the substrate's integration (§10); the workspace's capacity (§8: 400 tokens × 16 turns). Parents should stop trying to move these and read them as constants.

4. **Where the theories conflict, the child can arbitrate.** g (§1) vs multiple intelligences (§3): correlation of ON−OFF gains across gyms. Piaget's development-leads-learning (§4) vs Vygotsky's learning-leads-development (§5): whether lesson uptake is gated by prerequisite competence or whether a well-scaffolded lesson creates the competence. Both are cheap to read from ledgers we already keep.

5. **On modes (Rohin's ruling).** Dennett (§12) and Minsky (§11) make the strongest case that handing over tools, words and goals *is* how minds are built; Vygotsky (§5) says the parent's voice becomes the child's inner voice and so must be varied; Stanovich (§6) names offered rules "mindware" and says they are teachable separately from intelligence; Gardner & Hatch (§3) name multiple entry points. None of them supports "questions only"; all of them support "vary the mode and keep the child testing".

## Summary (10 lines)

1. Fourteen theories were checked from the shell (Crossref, PubMed, arXiv, Open Library, two primary PDFs); every record resolved; the abstract or the cited passage was read for the load-bearing paper of every theory except Gardner, Piaget, Vygotsky, Minsky and Hohwy (and Sternberg's 1985 book), whose records are confirmed and whose content is marked as recalled.
2. The theories split into three kinds: descriptions of what grows (CHC, Sternberg, Gardner, Piaget, Vygotsky, dual process), architectures with a learned part (predictive processing, global workspace, higher-order thought, Minsky, Dennett), and measures (Chollet, Legg & Hutter); IIT alone is pure substrate and teaches nothing — its use is to keep every sentence about the child behavioural.
3. On what intelligence IS they converge more than expected: turning priors and experience into skill efficiently on unseen environments, weighted toward breadth and simple regularities — which is the compiler-gym final test (taught vs untaught, identical mechanism, empty ledger) read as episodes-to-competence with the frozen model's first-contact skill subtracted.
4. Held fixed in a 7B child, by every theory that speaks to it: fluid ability at first contact (the base), the workspace's size (400 tokens × 16 turns), and whatever integration the substrate has; parents should read these as constants, not targets.
5. Teachable, and already in the corpus: the thinking moves (metacomponents; Type 2 intervention), predict-then-check and accommodation after surprise, internalised self-questions, metacognitive sensitivity read apart from score.
6. Teachable, and new to the corpus: workspace management ("if it is not written it does not exist for your next step"); precision ("which beliefs are you sure of, which are guesses?") and active inference ("when unsure, do the action that tells you most"); investment (turn a fresh solution into a stored rule); management over skill and negative knowledge ("what did you learn not to do?"); Occam as a disposition; Sternberg's adapt / shape / select triad.
7. The strongest support for Rohin's ruling comes from Dennett's Gregorian floor (minds grow by imported tools, kept honest by the Popperian habit of testing them), Minsky's imprimers (approval shapes goals, so spend it on values), Stanovich's mindware (offered rules are teachable and separate from intelligence), and Vygotsky (the parent's voice becomes the inner voice, so vary it): none supports "questions only".
8. Grain-of-salt absorption becomes countable as Gregorian uptake conditional on a tool's track record in the child's own ledger, disagreement rate, and whether disagreement is followed by a test; and Papert's principle tells us that gains from better selection among behaviours the base could already sample are legitimate growth, not a disappointment.
9. Two live conflicts the ledgers can arbitrate cheaply: g vs multiple intelligences (correlation of ON−OFF gains across gyms) and Piaget vs Vygotsky (whether lesson uptake is gated by prerequisite competence or created by scaffolding).
10. Nothing here has been measured on the child; every "evidence" line is a proposed instrument, and by IIT and Butlin et al. no such instrument bears on sentience — the paper says "appraisal language" and "indicator properties", never "feelings".
