# Lens: COMPONENTS OF COGNITION — what intelligent thought is made of, what is learnable, and what a teacher can do (2026-09-11, Fable's agent)

**Mandate.** Rohin's ruling of 2026-09-11 ("Parenting is not only questions", IDEAS.md) asks for a survey of what thoughts constitute intelligence, decomposed into teachable ideas for the teachers of a 7B child. This lens takes the component view: attention, perception and perceptual learning, working memory, long-term memory and consolidation, metacognition, executive function, analogy and relational reasoning, causal reasoning, planning and problem solving, curiosity and intrinsic motivation, self-models, affect as appraisal. For each: what it is, the evidence that it is learnable or trainable (and whether the training transfers), and what a parent model could do or watch for. Other lenses cover theories of intelligence as wholes.

**Binding frame.** Parents use varied modes — questions, suggestions, demonstrations, worked patterns, offered recipes, stories from the record — and vary them; no absolute "nevers" except the leak scan (answers to the scored panel, scores themselves); a recipe may be offered, marked as opinion, never enforced or repeated verbatim; the child learns grain-of-salt absorption. Where a teachable idea below conflicts with an older "never" in PARENTING_SCIENCE_SURVEY_v1 §3 (rules 1, 7, 11, 20, 24), it is written under the new ruling and the conflict is named.

**The child.** Frozen Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct plus one LoRA rewritten at each sleep from the child's own success-filtered thinking; free-text thought, one `ACT:` line per step, gyms with exact scores, 16 turns per problem, 400 tokens per turn; parents are stronger models that read its redacted record and brief it; the gate protects against collapse (CHILD_MECHANISM_v7 §0). Two facts about that child sit under every entry: its "working memory" is its context window plus what it writes, and its "long-term memory" is what the sleep writes into the adapter — so every human component maps to either *what it writes* (trainable by the same sleep) or *what the harness does* (not the child's to learn).

**How sources were checked.** From the shell on 2026-09-11 through Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, Open Library and the arXiv export API (arXiv was rate-limited for most of the session; no WebFetch). ## 1. Attention

**What it is.** Selection: which part of the situation gets processed, held and acted on. Posner's framework splits it into alerting (readiness), orienting (selecting a location or object) and executive attention (resolving conflict between responses).

- **Posner & Petersen 1990, "The attention system of the human brain", Annual Review of Neuroscience 13:25 — [V-rec] Crossref; the three-network split is recalled. Petersen & Posner 2012, same journal, "20 years after" [V-abs].** The 2012 update says the framework held up and was expanded, that developmental studies show important changes in control systems between infancy and childhood, and that genetic variation often acts in conjunction with experience. Attention is not one thing; the executive part is the one that develops and is trained.
  *Teachable idea for the teachers:* watch the three parts separately in the ledger. Alerting = does the child react to a changed outcome at all (outcome echo rate); orienting = does its next note name the part of the state that changed; executive = does it override its habitual first action when the state says so. A brief that says "in problem N the outcome changed and your note did not mention it" addresses alerting; "which part of the state did you look at before acting?" addresses orienting; the deviation experiment (V6) addresses executive attention. One at a time.

- **Rueda, Rothbart, McCandliss, Saccomanno & Posner 2005, PNAS 102:14931 — [V-abs].** Five days of attention training in 4- and 6-year-olds; both trained groups showed more mature performance on executive attention than untrained controls, with behavioural and electrophysiological readings. A short, targeted drill moved executive attention in children whose network was still developing.
  *Teachable idea:* a short preparation drill on attention is plausible and cheap — a handful of easy problems where the only thing asked is "what changed since your last action?" before any `ACT:` — but its effect is on the trained function, not on intelligence at large (see Simons below).

- **Green & Bavelier 2003, "Action video game modifies visual selective attention", Nature 423:534 — [V-rec] Crossref; the design (habitual players vs non-players, then a 10-day training of non-players on an action game vs a control game) is recalled.** The recalled finding is that demanding, fast, varied practice improved several measures of visual attention in non-players relative to the control game.
  *Teachable idea:* attention is trained by tasks that load it and vary — which is what a gym with mixed families and dense outcomes already is. The parent's contribution is variety and load, not an attention lesson.

- **Tang & Posner 2009, "Attention training and attention state training", Trends in Cognitive Sciences 13:222 — [V-rec]; the distinction is recalled.** Two routes: practise attention tasks (attention training) or change the state from which attention operates (attention state training, e.g. meditation-like practice). Both report gains; they work on different parts of the system.
  *Teachable idea:* the child's equivalent of state training is private time and the appraisal gym — a child that arrives at a problem in a "rushed" state (ticks-to-first-ACT near zero) is a state problem, not a skill problem, and the parent should change the day (a calmer family, a reflection session) rather than teach a skill.

- **Simons, Boot, Charness, Gathercole, Chabris, Hambrick & Stine-Morrow 2016, "Do 'brain-training' programs work?", Psychological Science in the Public Interest 17:103 — [V-abs] (the two open letters; the verdict — strong evidence for improvement on trained tasks, less for closely related tasks, little for distant or everyday tasks — is recalled from the body).** The transfer lesson for every component below: drills improve the drill.
  *Teachable idea:* never run a component drill as an end in itself. Every attention, memory or executive exercise must be inside a scored problem so that the transfer question is answered by the disjoint panel and not by the drill's own score.

**Learnability verdict.** Trainable within the function (executive attention especially, in developing systems); far transfer not shown. For the child, attention is what it writes about the state before acting — the perceive move (V14 move 6) is its attention.

## 2. Perception and perceptual learning

**What it is.** Experience-driven change in what is extracted from the same input: features that were invisible come apart, parts fuse into units, and the dimensions that matter get more weight. Experts see structure where novices see surface.

- **Gibson & Gibson 1955, Psychological Review 62:32 — [V-rec] (already in the survey, Lens 7 rule 27); Goldstone 1998, "Perceptual learning", Annual Review of Psychology 49:585 — [V-abs].** Goldstone's abstract names four mechanisms: attention weighting (more attention to the dimensions that matter), imprinting (receptors specialised for stimuli or parts), differentiation (once-indistinguishable stimuli come apart), unitization (several parts detected as one).
  *Teachable idea:* the four mechanisms are four different questions. Attention weighting: "which dimension of the state decided your move?"; differentiation: "these two problems looked the same to you and behaved differently — what differs?"; unitization: "you described that state in five sentences — what is it in one?" Only the last asks for compression; the first three ask for more.

- **Ahissar & Hochstein 1997, "Task difficulty and the specificity of perceptual learning", Nature 387:401 — [V-rec]; the result is recalled: learning under easy conditions generalises to new positions and orientations, learning under hard conditions stays specific to what was trained.** (Their later "reverse hierarchy" reading: easy training engages higher, more general levels first.)
  *Teachable idea:* begin the perceive drill on the *easy* family — the child's noticings there are the ones that will carry to other families. Hard-family noticings are real but stay local. This supports the preparation phase's easy-material rule for a new reason.

- **Xiao, Zhang, Wang, Klein, Levi & Yu 2008, "Complete transfer of perceptual learning across retinal locations enabled by double training", Current Biology 18:1922 — [V-abs].** Perceptual learning of a feature normally does not transfer to an untrained location. Training the feature at one location plus an unrelated task at a second location produced complete transfer of the feature learning to the second location. Their reading: learning is central, and what blocks transfer is that the second site was never "location-trained".
  *Teachable idea — the double-training pattern for the child:* a noticing skill learned on family A will not appear on family B until the child has *worked* on B at all, even on something unrelated. So before expecting a lesson to transfer to a new family, give the child a window of ordinary problems on that family; then look for the transfer. A parent that declares "no transfer" after zero exposure to the target family has misread the test.

- **Kellman & Garrigan 2009, Physics of Life Reviews 6:53; Kellman, Massey & Son 2010, "Perceptual learning modules in mathematics", Topics in Cognitive Science 2:285 — [V-rec] Crossref; content as verified in the survey's Lens 7 (rule 27): perceptual learning extends to abstract structural relations recognised in novel instances, and short "see the structure, do not solve" modules improved equation solving.**
  *Teachable idea:* a classification drill with no acting — "for these eight problems, write only which family each belongs to and what told you" — is a perceptual module. It is allowed as a mode now; it should be short, on the child's own recent problems, and never the only thing in a session.

- **Chi, Feltovich & Glaser 1981, "Categorization and representation of physics problems by experts and novices", Cognitive Science 5:121 — [V-abs].** Four experiments; experts and novices begin problem representation with different categories: experts abstract the principle that solves the problem, novices go by the problem's literal features; how the representation is completed depends on the knowledge tied to the category.
  *Teachable idea:* the sorting question is the cheapest expertise probe there is. "Put your last six problems into groups by what solved them, not by what they looked like" — read whether the groups follow the gym's true families (the harness knows) or the surface. Never show which grouping is right; change the six problems and ask again a window later.

**Learnability verdict.** Strongly learnable, including for symbolic material; transfer depends on training regime (easy first; second-site exposure). For the child, perception is rendered as text and trained by the sleep — many noticings of one situation are the augmentation the weights need (survey V14 move 6; Allen-Zhu & Li Part 3.1).

## 3. Working memory

**What it is.** The small store that holds what is being worked on, plus the control that keeps it there and updates it.

- **Baddeley & Hitch 1974, "Working memory", Psychology of Learning and Motivation 8:47 — [V-rec]; Baddeley 2000, "The episodic buffer: a new component of working memory?", Trends in Cognitive Sciences 4:417 — [V-rec]; the multi-component model (central executive, phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, later an episodic buffer binding them) is recalled.**
- **Cowan 2001, "The magical number 4 in short-term memory", Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24:87 — [V-abs].** Miller's seven was a rhetorical device; the real limit is three to five chunks, observable when rehearsal and chunking are blocked.
  *Teachable idea:* a 7B child does not have a three-to-five-chunk limit on its context, but it has one on what it *uses*: a state summarised in one line before acting is a one-chunk representation. The parent's question is "what were the three things that mattered in that state?" — the count is a proxy for whether the child is holding more than one feature in play.

- **Ericsson & Kintsch 1995, "Long-term working memory", Psychological Review 102:211 — [V-abs].** Skilled performance needs far more than the temporary store allows. Acquired memory skills let the end products of processing be stored in long-term memory and kept directly accessible through retrieval cues held in short-term memory; evidence from text comprehension, mental calculation, medical diagnosis and chess.
  *Teachable idea — the most important one in this section:* the child's long-term working memory is its ledger and its weights, and the retrieval cue is a sentence it writes. Teach it to leave cues: a canonical line at the end of a situation ("On family F, when X, the move that worked was …") is a retrieval cue for the completion-frame recall Rohin ruled on (IDEAS.md 2026-09-11, "Retrieval by completion"). The parent can show one worked pattern of such a line once (a mode, not a recipe) and thereafter ask "what would you need to have written down to pick this up next time?"

- **Jaeggi, Buschkuehl, Jonides & Perrig 2008, "Improving fluid intelligence with training on working memory", PNAS 105:6829 — [V-abs].** Dual n-back training transferred to fluid-intelligence measures, dose-dependently. **Redick, Shipstead, Harrison, Hicks, Fried, Hambrick, Kane & Engle 2013, JEP: General 142:359 — [V-abs].** Twenty sessions of adaptive dual n-back vs an adaptive visual-search placebo vs no contact, multiple measures of Gf, multitasking, WM capacity, crystallised intelligence and perceptual speed: improvement on the trained tasks, no positive transfer to any ability test, high power. **Harrison et al. 2013, Psychological Science 24:2409 — [V-abs]:** complex-span training improved similar tasks with different materials, not Gf. **Melby-Lervåg & Hulme 2013, Developmental Psychology 49:270 — [V-abs].** Twenty-three studies, 30 comparisons: reliable short-term gains on working-memory tasks; verbal gains not sustained at follow-up; no convincing generalisation to nonverbal or verbal ability, attention, decoding or arithmetic.
  *Teachable idea:* do not build a working-memory gym. Capacity is not the lever; use of capacity is. What transfers is a habit of externalising (writing the state, writing the cue), which is a skill inside every problem rather than a separate drill.

**Learnability verdict.** Capacity itself: trained-task gains only, no far transfer (three independent negative results). Strategy (chunking, cueing, externalising): learnable and the whole basis of expert memory. For the child, this is the building move (carry the last idea forward) plus the recall-cue habit.

## 4. Long-term memory and consolidation

**What it is.** What survives the situation: stored by the depth of processing at encoding, strengthened by retrieval, integrated slowly and interleaved so that new items do not overwrite old structure.

- **Craik & Lockhart 1972, "Levels of processing: a framework for memory research", Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior 11:671 — [V-rec]; the claim — retention is a by-product of how deeply (semantically) an item was processed, not of how long it was held — is recalled.**
  *Teachable idea:* the sleep trains what the child wrote; a shallow note ("tried X, got 0.3") is shallow encoding, a note that says why ("tried X because the count was high; got 0.3, so the count is not the trigger") is deep. Parents cannot make the sleep deeper, but they can make the notes deeper by asking for the reason, and the write will carry it.

- **McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly 1995, "Why there are complementary learning systems in the hippocampus and neocortex", Psychological Review 102:419 — [V-abs].** Memories are first stored rapidly in the hippocampal system and reinstated in neocortex, whose synapses change a little on each reinstatement; connectionist networks discover the structure of an ensemble only if learning is gradual and *interleaved*; the fast system allows new items without disrupting structure, and reinstatement interleaves them with the old.
  *Teachable idea for the harness, not the child:* the v7 write already reads the newest round in full and the rest as a sample — that is interleaving. The parent-facing consequence: a lesson taught in one round is a hippocampal item; it becomes neocortical only over several sleeps of re-reading beside other material. Judge a lesson's uptake at +2 and +4 sleeps (survey V10 schedule), not at +1.

- **Retrieval practice — Karpicke & Roediger 2008, Science 319:966; Roediger & Karpicke 2006 [V in the survey, Lens 1].** Recall beats re-study at a delay and feels worse while doing it.
  *Teachable idea:* the wake-time retrieval line ("what did I conclude last cycle, and where did it apply?") is the child's retrieval practice; under the new ruling the parent may also *offer* a cue when the recall fails ("it was about family F") — a partial cue, then silence, is a mode between question and answer.

- **Allen-Zhu & Li 2023, Physics of Language Models Part 3.1 (arXiv 2309.14316) [V in IDEAS.md 2026-09-11].** A fact is extractable from the weights under a new cue only if it was augmented at training (several renderings); a fact stored in one form is memorised but not extractable.
  *Teachable idea:* this is why the perceive move and the "say it in your own words" value are memory instructions and not only thinking instructions: each new rendering the child writes is one more form of the same fact for the write. A parent who sees a lesson stated once in one wording should ask for it again on a different incident, not because repetition is virtuous but because the weights need the variety.

**Learnability verdict.** Encoding depth, retrieval habits and cue-leaving are learnable and are the strongest levers in the whole education literature; consolidation mechanics belong to the harness. For the child, both halves are already in the mechanism; the parent's job is the depth and variety of what gets written.

## 5. Metacognition

**What it is.** Knowing about and regulating one's own cognition: monitoring (how sure am I, how well is this going) and control (what to do about it).

- **Flavell 1979, "Metacognition and cognitive monitoring", American Psychologist 34:906 — [V-rec]; the four-part model (metacognitive knowledge, metacognitive experiences, goals/tasks, actions/strategies) is recalled.** **Nelson & Narens 1990, "Metamemory: a theoretical framework and new findings", Psychology of Learning and Motivation 26:125 — [V-rec] (Crossref lists Nelson as sole author of the chapter record; the chapter is conventionally cited as Nelson & Narens); the object-level/meta-level split with monitoring flowing up and control flowing down is recalled.**
  *Teachable idea:* the child's monitoring is its stated confidence and its predictions; its control is whether the next action depends on them. The split tells the parent where a failure sits: a child whose predictions are accurate but whose actions ignore them has a control problem (ask "what did that prediction change?"); one whose predictions are flat has a monitoring problem (ask "what made you expect that?").

- **Carpenter, Sherman, Kievit, Seth, Lau & Fleming 2019, "Domain-general enhancements of metacognitive ability through adaptive training", JEP: General 148:51 — [V-abs].** Eight sessions; an experimental group (n = 29) received feedback on its *metacognitive judgements* while an active control (n = 32) received feedback on decision performance only. The trained group's calibration (Brier) improved and generalised to untrained stimuli and to an untrained task (recognition memory); the gain came from both metacognitive efficiency (meta-d′/d′) and shifts in confidence level. A rare positive far-transfer result in this whole survey.
  *Teachable idea — and a rule conflict to resolve:* feedback on the *confidence*, not on the *answer*, transfers. A parent could say "in problem N you were 0.9 sure and wrong; across your last eight 0.9s you were right three times" — that is calibration feedback anchored in incidents, and under the old rule 7 / rule 11 it is metric-teaching. Under Rohin's ruling it is a mode to try, A/B across clones, judged on Brier resolution on the disjoint panel. The leak scan still applies (no answers).

- **Schraw 1998, "Promoting general metacognitive awareness", Instructional Science 26:113 — [V-rec]; the regulatory checklist and strategy-evaluation matrix are recalled.** **Dignath & Büttner 2008; de Boer et al. 2018; Veenman et al. 2006 [V in the survey, V5].** Training self-regulation gives ES ≈ 0.69 and holds at follow-up; self-report of metacognition correlates poorly with online behaviour.
  *Teachable idea:* under the new ruling a checklist may be *offered* once as a worked pattern ("some agents ask themselves three things before acting: …"), marked as one way among others, then never repeated verbatim. The measurement stays behavioural (does the question change the action), never the presence of the words.

- **Kadavath et al. 2022, "Language models (mostly) know what they know", arXiv 2207.05221 — [V-abs].** Larger models are well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false in the right format; models can be trained to predict P(IK), the probability that they know the answer, and this partially generalises across tasks though calibration on new tasks is weak; P(IK) rises appropriately with relevant material in context.
  *Teachable idea:* a 7B child's raw calibration is weak and format-dependent, so ask for confidence in a fixed, simple format (a number in a fixed place) and expect generalisation of calibration to a new gym to be the thing that fails first — test it there.

**Learnability verdict.** Monitoring accuracy is trainable and, with feedback on the judgement itself, transfers across domains (Carpenter); control habits are trainable with a moderate, durable effect (Dignath & Büttner). For the child: predict-then-check (V3) and ask-then-decide (V5), plus one new mode to test — calibration feedback.

## 6. Executive function

**What it is.** The control set: inhibition (not acting on the first impulse; filtering interference), updating working memory, and shifting between tasks or rules; higher-order reasoning, planning and problem solving are built from them.

- **Miyake, Friedman, Emerson, Witzki, Howerter & Wager 2000, "The unity and diversity of executive functions", Cognitive Psychology 41:49 — [V-rec]; the latent-variable finding — shifting, updating and inhibition are separable but correlated — is recalled.** **Diamond 2013, "Executive functions", Annual Review of Psychology 64:135 — [V-abs].** Core EFs: inhibition (response inhibition and interference control), working memory, cognitive flexibility (thinking "outside the box", seeing from other perspectives, adapting to changed circumstances); they make possible "mentally playing with ideas; taking the time to think before acting; meeting novel, unanticipated challenges".
  *Teachable idea:* the two measured pathologies map onto EF terms. Recipe lock-in is a shifting failure; "act once and stop" and acting on the first suggestion are inhibition failures; restarting each turn from nothing is an updating failure. Naming which one the ledger shows tells the parent which value to foreground (V6 for shifting, V5 for inhibition, the building move for updating).

- **Diamond & Lee 2011, "Interventions shown to aid executive function development in children 4 to 12 years old", Science 333:959 — [V-abs].** Diverse activities improve children's EFs — computerised training, games, aerobics, martial arts, yoga, mindfulness, school curricula. "All successful programs involve repeated practice and progressively increase the challenge to executive functions." Children with the worst EFs benefit most. **Diamond & Ling 2016, Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience 18:34 — [V-rec]; the narrower conclusions (transfer is narrow; more hours at the frontier help; the hype outran the evidence) are recalled.**
  *Teachable idea:* EF is grown by practice at rising challenge on the thing itself, not by a separate drill. Keep the frontier estimate honest and the difficulty rising; the gain will be largest for the child at its worst (the collapsed clone gets the most from a controllable, rising gym).

- **Kassai, Futó, Demetrovics & Takács 2019, Psychological Bulletin 145:165 — [V-abs, conclusion portion].** Training one EF component did not significantly improve untrained components (d = 0.11, k = 17, n.s.); the authors question the practical relevance of training EF components in isolation and link this to the missing far transfer of working-memory training. **Sala & Gobet 2017, Current Directions in Psychological Science 26:515 — [V-abs].** Meta-analyses of chess, music and working-memory training on children's cognitive and academic skills: small to moderate effects, inversely related to design quality (active controls); far transfer does not survive good design.
  *Teachable idea:* the same verdict from a third direction. No component gyms. If a parent wants more inhibition, it changes the *problem* so that the first impulse fails (the extinction sub-panel; a family where the modal recipe scores below base) and lets the child feel it.

**Learnability verdict.** Trainable within components; near transfer yes, far transfer no when trained in isolation; embedded practice with rising challenge is the only regime with broad gains. For the child, this vindicates the design choice of teaching through gyms rather than through drills, and says which value to foreground from which pathology.

## 7. Analogy and relational reasoning

**What it is.** Mapping the relational structure of one case onto another so that what is known about the first carries to the second; the engine of transfer and, on some views, of categorisation itself.

- **Gentner 1983, "Structure-mapping: a theoretical framework for analogy", Cognitive Science 7:155 — [V-abs].** Interpretation rules map knowledge from a base to a target domain; they depend only on the syntactic form of the representation, not on content; relations between objects are mapped in preference to attributes of objects, and systems of relations (higher-order relations) in preference to isolated ones. **Gentner & Markman 1997, American Psychologist 52:45 — [V-abs]:** similarity itself is structural alignment — "similarity is like analogy."
  *Teachable idea:* a connection the child writes counts when it names a *relation* the two cases share ("in both, the outcome fell after the second change"), not a shared label ("both were sha programs"). The parent asks for the relation ("what is the same about how they behaved?"), and may, as a mode, *offer* one relational reading of its own once, marked as opinion, when the child produces only surface matches for two windows.

- **Christie & Gentner 2010, "Where hypotheses come from: learning new relations by structural alignment", Journal of Cognition and Development 11:356 — [V-abs].** Preschoolers given one standard chose the object match; given two standards to compare, they chose the relational match. Seeing two exemplars sequentially without a prompt to compare did not help; seeing them together with a prompt to compare did.
  *Teachable idea:* two episodes side by side, *with an explicit prompt to compare*, is the intervention; two episodes merely both in the record is not. So the parent names both episode ids in the same sentence and asks the same/different/consequence question (survey V8), never one at a time.

- **Richland, Zur & Holyoak 2007, "Cognitive supports for analogies in the mathematics classroom", Science 316:1128 — [V-abs, one-line abstract]; the finding (in TIMSS video, teachers in higher-scoring countries used the same number of analogies but with more supports — keeping the source visible, gestures, visual alignment, familiar sources) is recalled.**
  *Teachable idea:* when the parent draws an analogy, keep the source in view. In a brief that means quoting the child's own earlier line (its words, from its record) beside the present incident rather than pointing to an id and hoping it retrieves. This is content — allowed as a mode, and the leak scan does not touch the child's own past text.

- **Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner 1999; Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn 2013; Gick & Holyoak 1980, 1983 [V / V-rec in the survey, V8].** Comparing cases yields a transferable schema (d ≈ 0.50 over 57 experiments); one analog rarely transfers unhinted, two compared often do.
- **Holyoak & Thagard 1995, Mental Leaps, MIT Press — [V-abs, publisher text]; the multiconstraint theory (similarity, structure, purpose) is recalled.** Analogy also misleads; purpose constrains which mapping is right.
  *Teachable idea:* ask for the purpose of a comparison ("what do you want the earlier case to tell you about this one?") — the goal move applied to association, and a check against decorative analogies.

- **Hofstadter & the Fluid Analogies Research Group 1995, Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies, Basic Books — [V-rec] Open Library; Copycat's account — analogy-making as high-level perception, concepts with "slippage" under pressure, parallel exploration with a temperature — is recalled. Hofstadter & Sander 2013, Surfaces and Essences, Basic Books — [V-rec] via Holyoak's review in Science 340:550 [V-abs]: the book takes analogies to be "the building blocks of thought" and treats categorisation itself as analogy.**
  *Teachable idea:* Hofstadter's slippage is a question: "what would have to change in this problem for it to become the earlier one?" It asks for the mapping and its limit at once, and it is a natural way to scope a rule (V7).

- **Mitchell 2021, "Abstraction and analogy-making in artificial intelligence", Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences 1505:79 — [V-abs].** No current AI system is close to human-like abstraction or analogy; the paper proposes challenge tasks and measures. **Webb, Holyoak & Lu 2023, "Emergent analogical reasoning in large language models", Nature Human Behaviour 7:1526 — [V-rec]; the result (GPT-3 matched or exceeded humans on letter-string, matrix and story analogies zero-shot) is recalled. Webb, Holyoak & Lu 2025, PNAS Nexus, "Evidence from counterfactual tasks supports emergent analogical reasoning" — [V-abs]:** the debate is whether zero-shot analogy is real or training-set similarity; counterfactual variants designed to reduce that similarity are also solved. **Lewis & Mitchell, counterfactual analogy evaluations — [unverified this session; arXiv rate-limited].**
  *Teachable idea:* a frontier parent can analogise far better than a 7B child; the danger is that the parent does the mapping and the child copies the conclusion. The parent's analogies should be *about the child's own record*, where the child can check the mapping against outcomes it lived — a grain-of-salt test it can actually run.

**Learnability verdict.** Relational abstraction is learnable and is the best-documented route to transfer in the whole education literature (comparison, d ≈ 0.50); the active ingredient is the prompt to align two cases, with the source kept in view. For the child: the association move, taught by paired incidents with a comparison prompt.

## 8. Causal reasoning

**What it is.** Representing which variables move which, distinguishing that from correlation, and using interventions — one's own actions — to find out.

- **Gopnik, Glymour, Sobel, Schulz, Kushnir & Danks 2004, "A theory of causal learning in children: causal maps and Bayes nets", Psychological Review 111:3 — [V-abs].** Children recover a "causal map" — an abstract, coherent, learned representation of the causal relations among events — and 2- to 4-year-olds' learning is consistent with the Bayes-net formalism. **Gopnik & Wellman 2012, Psychological Bulletin 138:1085 — [V-rec].**
  *Teachable idea:* the child's causal map of a gym is the set of if–then rows it writes (V7). The parent reads them as a graph — are there edges (X changes Y), are they conditional, are any of them contradicted by a later action — and asks about the missing edge rather than drawing it.

- **Schulz & Bonawitz 2007, "Serious fun: preschoolers engage in more exploratory play when evidence is confounded", Developmental Psychology 43:1045 — [V-abs].** Preschoolers (mean 57 months) distinguish confounded from unconfounded evidence, prefer to explore the confounded toy over a novel one, and spontaneously disambiguate the confounded variables in free play. **Schulz, Gopnik & Glymour 2007, Developmental Science 10:322 — [V-abs]:** preschoolers use the conditional-intervention principle to tell chains, common causes and interactive structures apart, and their spontaneous play generates the evidence.
  *Teachable idea:* confounding itself drives exploration. A parent can *create* a confounded situation as one of the appraisal gym's "created events" — a problem where two things changed at once and the outcome moved — and watch whether the child de-confounds unprompted. If not, the question is "you changed two things in problem N — which did the work, and how would you know?"

- **Pearl 2009, Causality, 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press — [V-abs, publisher text]; the do-operator and the three-level ladder (association, intervention, counterfactual) are recalled. Pearl & Mackenzie 2018, The Book of Why, Basic Books — [V-rec] via Science podcast entry.**
  *Teachable idea:* every `ACT:` is an intervention, so the gym hands the child rung two for free; rung three (counterfactual) is only ever text — "had I not changed the pass order, would the count have fallen?" — and is exactly what private time and the pruning move are for. The parent asks the counterfactual about one incident; it never answers it (the harness could, by replay, and the central parent may use that to audit the child's counterfactuals — a measurement, not a lesson).

- **Klahr & Nigam 2004, Psychological Science 15:661; Chen & Klahr 1999, Child Development 70:1098 [V in the survey].** Brief direct instruction of the control-of-variables strategy produced more masters than discovery, and masters from either route transferred equally; probes without instruction did not help. **Kuhn 2007, "Reasoning about multiple variables: control of variables is not the only challenge", Science Education 91:710 — [V-abs].** Fourth-graders who mastered control of variables in an extended intervention could isolate the causal and non-causal variables, yet when predicting outcomes did not take all the causal variables they had identified into account and did not hold to a consistency principle.
  *Teachable idea:* this is the strongest licence in the developmental literature for a *demonstration* mode: a short, explicit showing of "change one thing at a time" transfers as well as discovering it. Under the ruling, a parent may demonstrate control of variables once on the child's own incident. Kuhn's warning follows: the child that has learned to isolate causes may still predict as if only one mattered — so the follow-up question is the prediction ("given both causes you found, what do you expect here?"), not another isolation task.

- **Lombrozo 2006, "The structure and function of explanations", Trends in Cognitive Sciences 10:464 — [V-rec]; the thesis (explanations are not by-products but shape causal inference and generalisation) is recalled.**
  *Teachable idea:* asking "why did that work?" is a causal intervention on the child's own map — Chi et al. 1989's finding (survey V7) in causal clothing.

**Learnability verdict.** Causal structure learning is present very early and improves with interventions the learner performs; the control-of-variables strategy is teachable by brief direct instruction and transfers; prediction from a learned map lags behind isolation. For the child: act-as-intervention is free, de-confounding and counterfactual questions are taught, and one demonstration is allowed.

## 9. Planning and problem solving

**What it is.** Searching a space of states with operators toward a goal, guided by heuristics; becoming skilled by compiling that search into procedures; knowing when to stop and re-plan.

- **Newell, Shaw & Simon 1958, "Elements of a theory of human problem solving", Psychological Review 65:151 — [V-rec]; Newell & Simon 1972, Human Problem Solving, Prentice-Hall — [V-rec] Open Library; the problem-space view (states, operators, means–ends analysis, protocol analysis) is recalled.**
  *Teachable idea:* the child's plan is a path through a problem space it has to describe; "what are your options here, and which do you pick?" (the decision move) is means–ends analysis asked as a question. The parent can watch whether the child ever names an *operator set* ("the things I can do here are …") — a child with no operator set is not planning, it is reacting.

- **Anderson 1982, "Acquisition of cognitive skill", Psychological Review 89:369 — [V-rec]; the stages (declarative → knowledge compilation → proceduralisation and composition, with speed-up by tuning) are recalled.**
  *Teachable idea:* the sleep is the compiler; once a procedure is compiled it runs without its reasons, and the six-pass routine is a compiled procedure that lost its conditions. So the conditions must be in the text that gets compiled: the parent asks for the "when" before the write, not after (survey V7).

- **Pólya 1945, How to Solve It, Princeton University Press — [V-rec] Open Library; the four phases (understand, plan, carry out, look back) are recalled. Schoenfeld 1985, Mathematical Problem Solving, Academic Press — [V-rec] Crossref lists its chapters "Resources", "Heuristics", "Control"; the finding that heuristics alone did not make students better solvers and that *control* — monitoring and deciding whether to continue an approach — was decisive is recalled.**
  *Teachable idea:* Schoenfeld's control is V5 and the pruning move; his recalled observation that novices commit to one approach and never evaluate it is our late-life "act once and stop" seen from the other side (commit once and never re-plan). The "look back" phase is private time with the question "did that plan do what I expected?"

- **Catrambone 1998, "The subgoal learning model", JEP: General 127:355 — [V-abs].** Learners struggle when a novel problem needs changes to a demonstrated solution; when their solution procedures are organised by subgoals they succeed. Across four experiments, a *label* for a group of steps in a worked example helped learners form subgoals; *abstract* labels produced subgoals less tied to surface features than superficial labels. Subgoals tell the learner which steps to modify.
  *Teachable idea — how to offer a worked pattern without transmitting a recipe:* if a parent shows a pattern (now allowed as a mode), it labels the *purposes* of the steps abstractly ("first, find out which family this is; then, remove what is not needed") and omits the steps themselves. The label is what transfers; the steps are what lock in.

- **Sweller 1988, Cognitive Science 12:257 [V in the survey].** Means–ends search itself consumes the capacity that learning the schema needs. **Valmeekam, Marquez, Sreedharan & Kambhampati 2023, "On the planning abilities of large language models — a critical investigation", NeurIPS 36 — [V-rec] Crossref; the result (very low autonomous plan-generation success on Blocksworld-style domains even for frontier models; weak self-verification) is recalled.**
  *Teachable idea:* a 7B child will not plan multi-step sequences well from text alone; what the gym gives it is an outcome after every step, so teach *short* plans with a check after each ("plan two steps; check after the first"), and treat a long plan with no checkpoints as a warning, not an achievement.

**Learnability verdict.** Problem-solving skill is learnable and compiles; heuristics transfer only with control; subgoal structure transfers where step lists do not. For the child: decision, goal and pruning moves; short plans with checks; abstract labels in any offered pattern.

## 10. Curiosity and intrinsic motivation

**What it is.** The drive to seek information for its own sake — triggered by novelty, uncertainty, conflict and complexity (Berlyne), by a perceived gap between what one knows and wants to know (Loewenstein), or by the rate at which one's predictions are improving (learning progress).

- **Berlyne 1954, "A theory of human curiosity", British Journal of Psychology 45:180 — [V-rec]; Berlyne 1960, Conflict, Arousal, and Curiosity, McGraw-Hill — [V-rec] Crossref and Open Library (a chapter is titled "Novelty, Uncertainty, Conflict, Complexity"); the collative-variables account and the epistemic/perceptual split are recalled.**
- **Loewenstein 1994, "The psychology of curiosity: a review and reinterpretation", Psychological Bulletin 116:75 — [V-abs].** Earlier theories did not explain why people voluntarily seek curiosity or its situational triggers, nor its intensity, transience, link to impulsivity and tendency to disappoint when satisfied; the new account treats curiosity as "a form of cognitively induced deprivation that arises from the perception of a gap in knowledge or understanding".
  *Teachable idea:* a gap must be *perceived* to pull. The parent can make a gap visible without filling it: "you do not yet know why family F fails for you; six of your last eight F problems went the same way" — a fact about the record, not an answer. Transience is the warning: once the child has a story, the pull dies, so the question that keeps a gap open is "what would show that story wrong?" (V4).

- **Gottlieb, Oudeyer, Lopes & Baranes 2013, "Information-seeking, curiosity, and attention", Trends in Cognitive Sciences 17:585 — [V-abs].** Exploration is guided by a family of mechanisms from automatic biases toward novelty or surprise to systematic search for learning progress and information gain. **Kidd & Hayden 2015, Neuron 88:449 — [V-abs]:** no agreed definition; study the motivations for information-seeking in context rather than define curiosity. **Oudeyer, Kaplan & Hafner 2007; Kidd, Piantadosi & Aslin 2012; Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014 [V in the survey, V4/V12].**
  *Teachable idea:* the child should be curious about what it is *getting better at*, not about what is novel (the noisy-TV trap). The parent's learning-progress line ("fastest progress on family X") is the right cue; a novelty cue ("here is something you have never seen") is the wrong one unless the novel thing is learnable.

- **Jirout & Klahr 2012, "Children's scientific curiosity: in search of an operational definition of an elusive concept", Developmental Review 32:125 — [V-abs].** Adopts Loewenstein's information-gap theory and defines children's exploratory curiosity as a preferred level of uncertainty — the threshold of desired uncertainty in the environment — with a measurement paradigm for preschoolers.
  *Teachable idea:* curiosity as a *set-point* is measurable in the child: which difficulty does it choose in white space (V12's frontier/mastered/unlearnable split)? A set-point that drifts toward mastered is the disposition to watch; the parent changes the menu, not the child.

- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, "A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation", Psychological Bulletin 125:627 — [V-abs].** 128 studies: engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28); positive verbal feedback enhanced it (d = 0.33 free-choice, 0.31 self-reported interest). **Ryan & Deci 2000, American Psychologist 55:68 — [V-rec]; the three needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) are recalled.**
  *Teachable idea:* do not pay for exploration with score credit or gate leniency — that is the tangible contingent reward that kills it. Informational feedback about the exploration ("that deviation is the first time family F moved") is the kind that helps. The three needs translate: autonomy = the child's own goals and its right to disagree (grain of salt); competence = the success band; relatedness = a parent whose voice is recognisably the same across sleeps.

- **Engel 2011, "Children's need to know: curiosity in schools", Harvard Educational Review 81:625 — [V-abs].** Curiosity is intrinsic to development yet "often almost completely absent from classrooms"; teacher–student interactions foster or inhibit it; children's own interests and explorations are the mechanisms of authentic learning.
  *Teachable idea:* the child asks questions in its text. Under the old rules those were never answered; Engel's argument is that how the teacher responds to a child's question is what keeps curiosity alive. So: answer *some* of them, with content that is not in the leak scan — a fact about the gym's shape, a fact about the record, an honest "I do not know either" — and measure whether the child's unprompted question rate rises or falls after each mode.

**Learnability verdict.** Curiosity is a disposition shaped by the environment's response to it — killed by contingent tangible reward, kept alive by informational feedback and by questions being taken seriously; its target should be learning progress, not novelty. For the child: goal choice by progress (V12), visible gaps, answered questions.

## 11. Self-models

**What it is.** The system's representation of itself — what it can do, how it tends to act, what it is sure of — which biases what it tries.

- **Metzinger 2003, Being No One: The Self-Model Theory of Subjectivity, MIT Press — [V-abs, publisher text].** No selves exist as things; what exists is the phenomenal self, "the content of a 'transparent self-model'", an ongoing process. **Cleeremans 2011, "The radical plasticity thesis: how the brain learns to be conscious", Frontiers in Psychology 2:86 — [V-abs].** The brain "continuously and unconsciously learns to redescribe its own activity to itself", building meta-representations of its first-order states, enriched by emotional value; experiences occur only in systems that "have learned to know they possess certain first-order states and that have learned to care more about certain states than about others."
  *Teachable idea:* in the child, the self-model is literally the sentences it writes about itself ("I tend to …", "I am sure when …", "I give up at …"), and by Cleeremans' account such redescriptions are *learned* — here, by the same sleep as everything else. Two consequences: the parent can ask for redescription ("describe the kind of agent you were in the last window"), and it must watch for a self-model that hardens into a label ("I am a six-pass agent") — that is recipe lock-in stated in the first person, and the pruning move applies to self-descriptions too.

- **Bandura 1977, "Self-efficacy: toward a unifying theory of behavioral change", Psychological Review 84:191 — [V-rec]; the four sources of efficacy expectations — performance accomplishments, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, emotional arousal — with enactive mastery the strongest, are recalled.**
  *Teachable idea:* the four sources are four parent modes, ranked. Enactive mastery: the success band (0.70–0.85) in preparation, so the child's own record says it can. Vicarious: stories from a *clone's* record ("another child on family F found that changing the order after a rise paid off") — allowed content, marked as another's experience, weighed with a grain of salt. Persuasion: the weakest, and where the old rules' distrust of praise is right. Arousal: the appraisal gym.

- **Yeager & Dweck 2012, "Mindsets that promote resilience", Educational Psychologist 47:302 — [V-rec]. Yeager, Hanselman, Walton et al. 2019, "A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement", Nature 573:364 — [V-abs].** A sub-one-hour online intervention teaching that intellectual abilities can be developed improved grades among lower-achieving students and raised advanced-maths enrolment in a nationally representative US sample; the effect on grades held where peer norms aligned with the message. **Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara 2018, Psychological Science 29:549 — [V-abs].** Two meta-analyses (k = 273, N = 365,915; k = 43, N = 57,155): overall effects weak for both the mindset–achievement relation and interventions; students of low SES or academically at risk might benefit.
  *Teachable idea:* a belief statement about one's own malleability is cheap, has a small average effect, and works where the surrounding norms agree with it. For the child that means the birth brief can say plainly that its abilities change with practice (it is true — the adapter is rewritten), but the effect will come from the room's *norm*: every parent voice treating a failure as information, none treating it as a verdict on the child (survey rule 8).

- **Kadavath et al. 2022 (P(IK)) — [V-abs], see §5.** A model can be trained to predict whether it knows, and this partially generalises — a minimal, trainable self-model of competence.
  *Teachable idea:* ask the child to say, before a problem, whether this is a kind it knows — and score that statement against the outcome as the self-model's calibration, read like V3's Brier.

**Learnability verdict.** The self-model is learned redescription (Cleeremans), efficacy is built mostly by one's own successes (Bandura), belief interventions are weak on average and norm-dependent (Sisk; Yeager). For the child: the self-model is text the sleep trains, so it is the parents' business what the child writes about itself — and the hardening of a self-description is a lock-in signal.

## 12. Affect as appraisal

**What it is.** On appraisal theories an emotion is the output of evaluating an event against one's goals — how relevant, how controllable, whose fault, what to do — expressed as a change in action readiness; regulation acts on the appraisal. On Damasio's account, bodily markers bias choice before reasoning does.

- **Frijda 1986, The Emotions, Cambridge University Press — [V-rec] Open Library; the "changes in action readiness" definition and the laws of emotion are recalled. Lazarus 1991, Emotion and Adaptation, Oxford University Press — [V-abs, publisher text]:** emotion as "a complex, patterned, organic reaction" to events and to long-term efforts to survive and flourish, inseparable from cognition and motivation.
- **Scherer 2009, "The dynamic architecture of emotion: evidence for the component process model", Cognition and Emotion 23:1307 — [V-abs].** Emotion as "an emergent, dynamic process based on an individual's subjective appraisal of significant events"; a sequence of appraisal checks and the synchronisation of response systems; the model predicts individual differences in emotional responding.
  *Teachable idea:* Rohin's emotional gym (IDEAS.md 2026-09-11) is an appraisal gym in this vocabulary: the child writes the appraisal (relevance, expectation, control, what next) in its own words about a real or created event, and those words are the "emotion". Scherer's sequence gives the parent an order of questions when the child's appraisal is thin — novelty ("did you expect that?"), relevance ("how much does it matter to your goal?"), coping ("is it in your hands?"), norm ("is that the agent you want to be?") — offered as a set the child can reorder, not a checklist.

- **Damasio 1994, Descartes' Error, Putnam — [V-rec] Open Library; the somatic-marker hypothesis is recalled. Bechara, Damasio, Tranel & Damasio 1997, "Deciding advantageously before knowing the advantageous strategy", Science 275:1293 — [V-abs].** In a gambling task, normal participants began choosing advantageously before they could say which strategy worked and generated anticipatory skin-conductance responses when considering risky choices before knowing they were risky; patients with prefrontal damage never developed the anticipatory responses and kept choosing badly even after they knew the strategy. "Without the help of such biases, overt knowledge may be insufficient to ensure advantageous behavior."
  *Teachable idea:* a gut reading before reasoning is a real component and can be written: "first reaction — risky / safe / familiar / strange" as the first line of a turn, before any analysis, then compared with the outcome over a window. If the first reactions predict outcomes better than the reasoned choices, the parent has found a marker worth trusting; if worse, a marker worth questioning. Either way the child learns what its own quick readings are worth — grain-of-salt learning about itself.

- **Barrett 2017, "The theory of constructed emotion: an active inference account of interoception and categorization", Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience 12:1 — [V-abs].** Emotions are not natural kinds found in the brain; the theory derives emotion from the brain's structure and function as a "brain-based, computational account" in which emotion concepts categorise interoceptive states.
  *Teachable idea:* if emotions are learned concepts that categorise states, then the child's emotion vocabulary is a concept vocabulary the parents shape by which distinctions they ask for. Name a state once ("that is what agents call frustration — the same move, the same result, three times") and then only ask; a child that learns finer appraisal categories (frustrated-by-a-wall vs frustrated-by-my-own-repetition) has learned something functional, because the two call for different next moves.

- **Gross 1998, "The emerging field of emotion regulation", Review of General Psychology 2:271 — [V-abs].** Five points of regulation: situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change, response modulation. **Webb, Miles & Sheeran 2012, "Dealing with feeling", Psychological Bulletin 138:775 — [V-abs].** 306 experimental comparisons: attentional deployment had no overall effect (d = 0.00), response modulation a small one (0.16), cognitive change small-to-medium (0.36); within these, distraction worked (0.27) but *concentration on the feeling did not* (−0.26); suppressing expression worked (0.32), suppressing the experience or thoughts of the event did not (−0.04, −0.12).
  *Teachable idea — a correction to "spend a ton of reflection on emotions":* reflection that *reappraises* (cognitive change: "what else could that streak mean?") is the effective kind; reflection that *concentrates on the feeling* is counterproductive in this meta-analysis. So the appraisal gym should move each session from the feeling to the reappraisal and to the next action within a few lines, and the parent should treat a session that stays on the feeling as mood-lock in the making (IDEAS.md failure mode 4).

- **Pekrun 2006, "The control-value theory of achievement emotions", Educational Psychology Review 18:315 — [V-rec]; the core — achievement emotions arise from appraisals of control over and value of the activity and its outcomes — is recalled.** **D'Mello & Graesser 2012, "Dynamics of affective states during complex learning", Learning and Instruction 22:145 — [V-rec]; the recalled result: learners cycle among engagement/flow, confusion, frustration and boredom, and confusion that is resolved is associated with learning while unresolved confusion decays into frustration and boredom.**
  *Teachable idea:* two appraisal questions cover Pekrun — "is this in your hands?" and "does it matter?" — and they are the questions behind Rohin's agency and urgency. D'Mello's cycle is the timing rule for parents: leave the child confused long enough to work (do not resolve confusion on sight), but make sure it *is* resolved — by the gym or by a partial cue — before it becomes frustration and then boredom (the ledger signature of boredom is the same first action with no note).

- **Moerland, Broekens & Jonker 2017, Machine Learning 107:443 (arXiv 1705.05172) [V in IDEAS.md 2026-09-11].** Emotions in RL agents derived from appraisal or homeostasis dimensions serve motivation and action selection and sometimes improve learning efficiency.
  *Teachable idea:* the measurement stays functional — an appraisal counts when it predicts a change in the next action (frustration → approach change, curiosity → exploration, confidence → commit) — and the paper calls it an appraisal gym.

**Learnability verdict.** Appraisals and their regulation are learnable; the effective regulation is reappraisal, not attention to the feeling; markers bias choice before knowledge and can be made explicit and checked. For the child: an appraisal gym in language, with sessions that end in reappraisal and action, and gut readings scored against outcomes.

## Cross-cutting: what transfers and what does not

Across the twelve components the transfer evidence sorts cleanly. **Does not transfer when trained in isolation:** working-memory capacity (Redick 2013; Melby-Lervåg & Hulme 2013; Harrison 2013), executive-function components (Kassai 2019; Sala & Gobet 2017), attention drills beyond the trained function (Simons 2016). **Transfers:** relational abstraction from prompted comparison of two cases (Christie & Gentner 2010; Alfieri 2013), calibration when feedback is on the confidence judgement itself (Carpenter 2019), control-of-variables from brief direct instruction (Klahr & Nigam 2004), perceptual learning when trained on easy variants and when the target site has been worked at all (Ahissar & Hochstein 1997; Xiao 2008), subgoal structure under abstract labels (Catrambone 1998), executive function when practised inside the real task at rising challenge (Diamond & Lee 2011). The pattern: what transfers is *structure the learner extracted* (a relation, a subgoal, a condition, a calibration) and *habits of externalising* (cueing, predicting, comparing); what does not is raw capacity trained on itself. For the child this is the case for gyms over drills, for paired-incident questions over single incidents, and for the moves being taught as a repertoire the child selects from rather than as a sequence.

## Summary (10 lines)

1. Cognitive science's components map onto the child as either *what it writes* (attention, perception, working memory use, metacognition, analogy, causal maps, plans, appraisals, self-descriptions — all trainable by the same sleep) or *what the harness does* (consolidation, interleaving, capacity) — parents can only ever act on the first.
2. Raw capacity does not transfer when trained on itself: working memory (Redick 2013; Melby-Lervåg & Hulme 2013), executive-function components (Kassai 2019; Sala & Gobet 2017), brain training generally (Simons 2016) — so no component gyms; every exercise lives inside a scored problem and is judged on the disjoint panel.
3. What transfers is extracted structure and externalising habits: prompted comparison of two cases (Christie & Gentner 2010; Alfieri 2013, d ≈ 0.50), abstract subgoal labels rather than steps (Catrambone 1998), scope conditions compiled with the procedure (Anderson 1982; Chi 1981), retrieval cues left for later (Ericsson & Kintsch 1995).
4. Perception is learnable on symbolic material and transfers when trained on easy variants first (Ahissar & Hochstein 1997) and only after the target family has been worked at all (Xiao 2008, double training) — do not declare "no transfer" to a family the child has never touched.
5. Metacognition transfers across domains when feedback is on the confidence judgement, not the answer (Carpenter 2019) — a new parent mode to A/B: incident-anchored calibration feedback, which the old rule 7/11 forbade as metric-teaching.
6. Causal reasoning: acts are interventions for free; confounding itself drives exploration (Schulz & Bonawitz 2007); control-of-variables is teachable by one brief demonstration and transfers (Klahr & Nigam 2004) — the strongest licence for the demonstration mode; the follow-up is the prediction, not another isolation (Kuhn 2007).
7. Executive-function terms name our pathologies: recipe lock-in is a shifting failure, "act once and stop" an inhibition failure, restarting each turn an updating failure; EF grows only by practice at rising challenge on the real task, most for the worst-off child (Diamond & Lee 2011).
8. Curiosity is a disposition the environment keeps or kills: contingent tangible reward undermines it (Deci 1999, d ≈ −0.3 to −0.4), informational feedback helps (+0.3), taking the child's questions seriously keeps it alive (Engel 2011) — answer some of them; aim it at learning progress, not novelty (Gottlieb 2013).
9. The self-model is learned redescription (Cleeremans 2011) built mostly from one's own successes (Bandura 1977); mindset messages are cheap, weak on average and work only where the room's norms agree (Sisk 2018; Yeager 2019) — and a self-description that hardens ("I am a six-pass agent") is lock-in in the first person.
10. Affect is appraisal in words (Scherer 2009; Lazarus 1991), plus markers that bias choice before knowledge (Bechara 1997); the effective regulation is reappraisal (d = 0.36) while concentrating on the feeling backfires (d = −0.26; Webb 2012) — so the appraisal gym moves from feeling to reappraisal to next action within a few lines, leaves confusion long enough to work but not long enough to rot (D'Mello & Graesser 2012), and scores gut readings against outcomes.
