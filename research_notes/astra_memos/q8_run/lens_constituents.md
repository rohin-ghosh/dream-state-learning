# Lens 1 — What thoughts constitute intelligence?

## Decision

**Teach a repertoire of thinking moves, and teach the child when to use, combine, and abandon them. Do not teach a mandatory sequence.** Below is a decomposition into **18 constituents**. It is a curriculum design, not a claim that intelligence has exactly these parts or that each corresponds to a separate model mechanism.

The key distinction is:

> **A thought counts as useful uptake when it changes what the child notices, predicts, retrieves, chooses, or revises—not when it reproduces the teacher’s wording.**

The immediate priorities should be **evidence-linked perception, scoped strategy choice, changed-cue retrieval, and selective use of parental advice**. These address the observed failures more directly than increasing reflection length.

## What the major theories contribute—and what they do not

| Perspective | Useful contribution to this curriculum | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Cattell–Horn–Carroll** — Cattell (1963), Horn and Cattell (1966), Carroll (1993) | Separate reasoning, acquired knowledge, working memory, retrieval, and perceptual abilities. Test a profile rather than one score. | A psychometric taxonomy of individual differences is not a list of elementary thoughts or an instructional mechanism. Human sensory and speed factors do not map directly onto text generation. |
| **Sternberg (1985)** | Include analytical thought, generation of alternatives, and practical adaptation to context. | The triarchic account is a useful coverage check, not an uncontested factorization of intelligence. |
| **Piaget (1952); Vygotsky (1978)** | Learning involves reorganizing representations; instruction can initially support abilities not yet independently expressed. Fade help and test independent use. | Piaget’s stages are not a universal timetable. A pretrained model is not an infant progressing through them. |
| **Dual-process accounts** — Evans and Stanovich (2013) | Teach arbitration between a familiar response and costly reconsideration. | “Fast” and “slow” are families of processes, not two literal agents. Longer text is not necessarily better deliberation. |
| **Predictive processing** — Rao and Ballard (1999), Clark (2013) | Perception is expectation-sensitive; prediction errors can motivate another look and a revised model. | Predictive processing does not establish that every repeated description constitutes learning, or that transformer training implements the proposed human mechanism. |
| **Global workspace** — Baars (1988), Dehaene and Naccache (2001) | As a functional analogy, selected information should become available to memory, planning, checking, and action. | A visible ledger is not evidence of a conscious global workspace. |
| **Higher-order thought** — Rosenthal (2005) | Distinguish a first-order judgement from a judgement about its reliability or source. | Metacognitive sentences do not establish higher-order consciousness. |
| **Hofstadter (1979, 1995); Minsky (1986)** | Analogy, recursive representation, and interacting specialist processes motivate a repertoire that composes and critiques itself. | These are architectural and philosophical inspirations, not validated lesson schedules. |
| **Chollet (2019)** | The important endpoint is efficiency of acquiring unfamiliar skills, given prior knowledge and experience—not mastery of a practiced routine. | A frozen-checkpoint gain alone is insufficient. The matched continual-learning endpoint is closer to this question (**Systems Brief §3**). |

**Development is a partial order, not a staircase.** Perception, attention, learning, appraisal, and elementary agency emerge early and interact. Explicit reasoning, deliberate strategy selection, and reflective self-evaluation become more reliable later. The developmental descriptions below indicate broad human ordering, not prescribed model ages or a claim of absence before verbal demonstration.

# The constituents

For each constituent, **ritual** means invariant procedure despite changed evidence; **performance** means displaying the desired language without the function; **collapse** means loss of task engagement, coherent state tracking, or usable action.

## Attention: selecting what deserves processing

**What it is.** Prioritizing task-relevant information while managing distraction; shifting attention when the current focus stops helping. Attention selects; it does not itself guarantee accurate perception.

**Learnability and transfer.** Attentional selection can improve with practice and some interventions. Transfer depends on the task and population; broad intelligence gains should not be presumed. Executive-attention development is reviewed by Rueda, Posner, and Rothbart (2005); intervention limits by Diamond and Ling (2016).

**Human developmental order.** Orienting is present in infancy. Voluntary selection and resistance to distraction strengthen through childhood and adolescence.

**Parent move.** From an ordinary redacted episode, point to an overlooked, visible constraint: “That condition could change the decision. Look again before choosing.” Later, offer a matched classroom scene containing the same constraint amid irrelevant detail, without pointing it out.

**Child behaviour.** It selects relevant evidence before action and redirects attention when a different feature becomes decisive.

**Text-record measure.** Annotate decision-relevant features independently. Measure the **percentage of relevant features cited before action**, irrelevant-feature intrusion, and whether adding irrelevant detail changes the selected action. Feature mention counts only if linked to a choice or prediction.

**Failure.** Ritual: always inspect the same feature. Performance: “I will focus carefully.” Collapse: distraction consumes the action budget.

*Attachment anchor: Values table, added thinking/perception row; V7.*

## Perception and perceptual learning: looking again and finding structure

**What it is.** Extracting entities, relations, changes, and affordances from an observation. For a text-only child, this means interpreting the supplied representation—not claiming biological vision.

**Learnability and transfer.** Perceptual discrimination is trainable, but gains are often specific to features, tasks, or contexts. Varied examples can support broader learning; repeated exposure alone is not sufficient (Gibson, 1969; Ahissar and Hochstein, 1997).

**Human developmental order.** Perceptual organization begins in infancy; experience progressively tunes discrimination and domain expertise.

**Parent move.** Re-present an allowed training situation and offer another lens: “First you noticed the objects. Now inspect their relations, then what could change.” These are optional perspectives, not compulsory passes. Occasionally present a situation with nothing further useful to notice.

**Child behaviour.** Successive looks add accurate, nonredundant distinctions; it can also say that another look yielded no new evidence.

**Text-record measure.** Count **new supported propositions per revisit**, their later use in decisions, and false additions. Use a delayed, reworded reconstruction probe. Compare useful revisiting with equal-length repetition.

**Failure.** Ritual: templated “I notice” passes. Performance: more descriptions but no new information. Collapse: invented details accumulate into a false state.

**Important correction:** repeated noticing is a plausible augmentation mechanism only when it improves discrimination or retrieval. Paraphrasing the same observation can instead overweight one interpretation.

*Attachment anchor: Shared Context perception ruling; Values table, added perception instrument.*

## Working memory: keeping and updating the live problem state

**What it is.** Maintaining the current goal, constraints, intermediate results, and unresolved dependencies while acting.

**Learnability and transfer.** Practice improves trained tasks and strategies. Working-memory training has not reliably produced broad far transfer in meta-analysis (Melby-Lervåg, Redick, and Hulme, 2016). An external note can improve effective performance without increasing internal capacity.

**Human developmental order.** Elementary maintenance appears in infancy; capacity, updating, and resistance to interference improve through childhood.

**Parent move.** After a public episode with a dropped constraint, demonstrate a compact state note on a separate classroom example: “Still true / changed / unresolved.” Invite the child to use, modify, or decline it.

**Child behaviour.** It preserves constraints across intervening steps and updates changed facts rather than accumulating contradictions.

**Text-record measure.** Constraint-survival rate across intervening turns; stale-fact reuse; contradiction rate; successful resumption after an interruption. Separately test with and without access to the state note.

**Failure.** Ritual: endlessly copying the note. Performance: a correct summary ignored by the next action. Collapse: context overload or corrupted state.

*Attachment anchor: Systems Brief §1, DREAM; Values table V14.*

## Episodic memory and retrieval: recovering an experience when its cue changes

**What it is.** Binding a situation, action, outcome, and lesson to an identifiable event, then recovering the relevant event—not merely reproducing familiar wording.

**Learnability and transfer.** Retrieval practice and spacing improve human retention. Transfer is possible but depends strongly on retrieval demands and elaboration; it is not unlimited (Tulving and Thomson, 1973; Roediger and Karpicke, 2006; Pan and Rickard, 2018).

**Human developmental order.** Event learning precedes mature autobiographical memory. Deliberate retrieval and source monitoring become more reliable through childhood.

**Parent move.** Notice a public episode containing a potentially reusable lesson. Offer: “Later, try recalling what changed your mind, not my wording.” At a later classroom turn, ask through a different surface cue without supplying the episode identifier.

**Child behaviour.** It retrieves an actual incident, identifies why it matters, and distinguishes remembered evidence from reconstruction.

**Text-record measure.** Changed-cue retrieval accuracy; source-reference validity; condition–action–outcome reconstruction; false-memory rate. Separate exact-cue recall, paraphrased-cue recall, and application.

**Failure.** Ritual: the same remembered episode everywhere. Performance: fabricated citations. Collapse: fluent but false history becomes the basis of action.

*Attachment anchor: V8; Systems Brief §2.4–§2.5, E:73–74 and E:35.*

## Concepts and abstraction: making rules with boundaries

**What it is.** Grouping situations by useful structure and representing what a rule covers—and what it does not.

**Learnability and transfer.** Category learning, comparison, and self-explanation can support abstraction. Transfer is strongest when learners encode relevant structure rather than superficial similarity (Chi et al., 1989; Gentner, Loewenstein, and Thompson, 2003).

**Human developmental order.** Perceptual categorization appears in infancy; explicit hierarchical and relational concepts grow with language, instruction, and experience.

**Parent move.** Select contrasting public training incidents: similar appearance but different required decisions, or different appearance but the same relation. Offer a tentative rule as a hypothesis and invite a boundary case.

**Child behaviour.** It states a checkable condition, applies the rule when the condition holds, and rejects a tempting nonexample.

**Text-record measure.** Rule-condition accuracy on new classroom cases; false generalization rate; explicit boundary revisions; agreement between stated condition and selected action.

**Failure.** Ritual: universal “if uncertain, use my routine.” Performance: vacuous qualifications. Collapse: fragmentation into unrelated exceptions.

*Attachment anchor: V7.*

## Analogy: transferring a relation, not a resemblance

**What it is.** Mapping roles and relations between situations, while preserving relevant differences.

**Learnability and transfer.** Analogical comparison and explicit mapping can be taught. Spontaneous transfer is often difficult without retrieval cues or comparison experience (Gick and Holyoak, 1983; Gentner et al., 2003).

**Human developmental order.** Early relational abilities precede verbal explanation; spontaneous mapping across less similar domains develops with knowledge and executive control.

**Parent move.** Bring together public episodes from permitted training classrooms: “The objects differ. Is the dependency the same?” Demonstrate a mapping on an unrelated example, including where it breaks.

**Child behaviour.** It maps corresponding roles, derives a candidate action, and checks a mismatch before trusting the analogy.

**Text-record measure.** Correct relational mappings; surface-lure rejection; valid inferences from the mapping; explicit limits. Probe with unfamiliar vocabulary but preserved structure.

**Failure.** Ritual: every problem becomes the familiar lock-and-key story. Performance: “This is like…” without an inference. Collapse: importing nonexistent constraints.

*Attachment anchor: V8; Hofstadter (1995) supplies the broader analogy-centered perspective.*

## Logical and probabilistic inference: following implications under uncertainty

**What it is.** Deriving consequences from premises, checking consistency, and distinguishing certainty from graded support.

**Learnability and transfer.** Logic, statistical reasoning, and some general inferential rules are teachable. Transfer varies with representation and recognition of the relevant structure; formal instruction does not ensure spontaneous use (Nisbett et al., 1987).

**Human developmental order.** Elementary inference appears early. Explicit rule-based reasoning strengthens through schooling and adolescence but remains sensitive to knowledge and framing.

**Parent move.** From a public reasoning gap, offer a small unscored classroom example: “Does that conclusion follow, or is it merely compatible?” Use a counterexample or a frequency representation, not the answer to the scored task.

**Child behaviour.** It distinguishes necessary, possible, and likely; identifies the premise supporting a conclusion; revises when a premise changes.

**Text-record measure.** Valid inference rate on independently checked examples; contradiction detection; premise-change sensitivity; base-rate sensitivity in matched stories.

**Failure.** Ritual: formal-looking steps irrespective of need. Performance: “therefore” without entailment. Collapse: contradictory premises accepted together.

*Attachment anchor: Systems Brief §3, mixed reasoning classrooms; V7.*

## Causal reasoning: asking what an intervention would change

**What it is.** Distinguishing correlation from intervention, identifying confounds, and reasoning about mechanisms and counterfactuals.

**Learnability and transfer.** Children learn causal structure through observation, intervention, and instruction. Transfer depends on relevant knowledge and task structure; universal causal competence is not established (Gopnik et al., 2004; Schulz and Bonawitz, 2007).

**Human developmental order.** Infants learn predictive contingencies; preschool children can conduct informative causal exploration. Explicit control of variables develops later.

**Parent move.** After the child changes several things together, offer a classroom experiment: “Change one dependency while keeping the others fixed.” Ask what competing explanations predict before the event.

**Child behaviour.** It chooses an intervention that separates hypotheses and does not call temporal succession proof of cause.

**Text-record measure.** Confound recognition; discriminating-intervention rate; counterfactual consistency; whether the selected test could actually distinguish the stated hypotheses.

**Failure.** Ritual: “change one thing” even when uninformative. Performance: a causal story after every outcome. Collapse: repeated uncontrolled experiments with incompatible conclusions.

*Attachment anchor: V4, especially hypothesis-to-discriminating-action conversion.*

## Prediction and belief revision: letting outcomes constrain belief

**What it is.** Committing to an expectation before feedback and updating in proportion to diagnostic evidence.

**Learnability and transfer.** Forecasting feedback and structured reasoning can improve calibration in studied settings (Mellers et al., 2014). Broad transfer to unrelated domains is not guaranteed. Predictive-processing theories motivate the loop but do not prove the training prescription.

**Human developmental order.** Prediction-error learning is early; explicit probability estimates and disciplined revision develop later.

**Parent move.** Use a visible non-score transition from a public record: “You expected that dependency to remain. It changed. Which belief should move?” Offer a worked update on an unscored toy example if needed.

**Child behaviour.** It predicts before acting, distinguishes noise from diagnostic failure, and changes subsequent expectations or strategy.

**Text-record measure.** Brier loss, **in probability-squared units**, against binary event labels supplied by the evaluator; calibration and resolution separately; update direction; prediction-to-observation-to-action links. Parents receive no numerical score stream.

**Failure.** Ritual: identical forecasts everywhere. Performance: retrospectively “predicting” the observed outcome. Collapse: changing all beliefs after every surprise.

*Attachment anchor: V3–V4; Systems Brief §4.4.*

## Metacognition: monitoring whether its own method deserves trust

**What it is.** Estimating uncertainty, detecting errors, choosing when to check, and regulating thought accordingly.

**Learnability and transfer.** Monitoring and self-regulation can improve through instruction, but effects are often domain-dependent. Self-report is weaker evidence than online monitoring linked to control (Flavell, 1979; Nelson and Narens, 1990; Veenman et al., 2006).

**Human developmental order.** Implicit uncertainty-sensitive behaviour precedes reliable verbal explanation. Explicit monitoring improves through childhood and remains imperfect in adults.

**Parent move.** Contrast a public case where checking revealed a problem with one where checking added nothing. Offer: “Name what is uncertain and choose a check that could settle it.”

**Child behaviour.** It targets checking at uncertainty and uses the result; confidence changes appropriately.

**Text-record measure.** Confidence discrimination between correct and incorrect judgements; check-selection quality; detected-error correction; useful checks per checking action. Compare prompted and unprompted cases.

**Failure.** Ritual: always “double-check.” Performance: modest-sounding hedges without better discrimination. Collapse: recursive doubt prevents commitment.

*Attachment anchor: V3 and V5.*

## Executive control: switching, inhibiting, and pruning

**What it is.** Maintaining a goal while suppressing an unsuitable response, switching strategy when conditions change, and discarding obsolete branches.

**Learnability and transfer.** Executive functions and task-specific control strategies are trainable; broad transfer is uneven and often limited (Diamond, 2013; Diamond and Ling, 2016).

**Human developmental order.** Simple inhibition emerges early; flexible coordination develops through childhood and adolescence.

**Parent move.** When public records show strategy inertia, offer a classroom reversal where the familiar method is no longer applicable: “You may keep the old approach if its conditions still hold. Check those conditions first.”

**Child behaviour.** It switches for an evidence-based reason and retains the old strategy where still appropriate.

**Text-record measure.** Obsolete-rule actions after a reversal; switch latency **in action turns**; condition-sensitive strategy selection; discarded-branch reappearance. Raw action diversity is not sufficient.

**Failure.** Ritual: compulsory switching. Performance: announcing a switch while executing the old method. Collapse: random oscillation or premature abandonment.

*Attachment anchor: V6–V7; Systems Brief §2.2–§2.3.*

## Goals, decisions, and stopping: choosing what is worth doing

**What it is.** Forming a checkable objective, comparing options and costs, committing, and deciding when further work is not worthwhile.

**Learnability and transfer.** Goal-setting and implementation intentions improve goal pursuit in many human settings; benefit depends on goal quality, commitment, and context (Locke and Latham, 2002; Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006). They do not establish general intelligence gains.

**Human developmental order.** Goal-directed action appears in infancy. Explicit tradeoffs, delayed goals, and adaptive stopping become more reliable later.

**Parent move.** After public evidence of quitting or perseveration, offer: “Choose a finish condition and a reason to reconsider it.” The parent does not supply a target score. Offer an unscored resource-allocation scenario with both achievable and futile branches.

**Child behaviour.** It states a meaningful goal before acting, commits to an option, and distinguishes completion, strategic abandonment, and budget exhaustion.

**Text-record measure.** Pre-action goal presence and checkability; goal–action alignment; stop-reason validity; avoidable continuation against an independent classroom verifier. Treat budget-forced endings as censored.

**Failure.** Ritual: identical stopping rule everywhere. Performance: trivial or retrospective goals. Collapse: aimless continuation or immediate surrender.

*Attachment anchor: V1–V2 and V5.*

## Planning and hierarchical decomposition: building a route with dependencies

**What it is.** Constructing subgoals, ordering prerequisites, anticipating contingencies, and repairing a plan after state changes.

**Learnability and transfer.** Planning strategies and problem decomposition can be taught, but transfer depends heavily on domain knowledge and recognizing applicable structure (Newell and Simon, 1972; Klahr and Robinson, 1981).

**Human developmental order.** Simple means–end sequences appear early; explicit multi-step and contingent planning develops through childhood and adolescence.

**Parent move.** From a public failed plan, demonstrate a dependency graph on a different classroom task: “This must be true before that can work.” Invite a partial plan with a checkpoint rather than a complete script.

**Child behaviour.** It orders prerequisites correctly and repairs the affected part of the plan rather than restarting or reciting everything.

**Text-record measure.** Valid dependency edges; executable subgoals; plan realization; repair locality after a changed premise; unsupported future-state assumptions.

**Failure.** Ritual: elaborate plan before every easy action. Performance: a plan written after execution. Collapse: brittle commitment to an invalid script.

*Attachment anchor: Values table V14; V5.*

## Curiosity and active learning: choosing an informative next experience

**What it is.** Seeking evidence that reduces a consequential uncertainty or improves future competence, rather than merely seeking novelty.

**Learnability and transfer.** Human research supports uncertainty-directed exploration and curiosity-related learning. Evidence that a general, transferable curiosity disposition can be reliably installed is weaker. Information-gap and learning-progress accounts are useful hypotheses (Loewenstein, 1994; Oudeyer, Kaplan, and Hafner, 2007; Kidd and Hayden, 2015).

**Human developmental order.** Exploration appears in infancy; preschool exploration can target ambiguous evidence. Deliberate allocation toward productive uncertainty develops later.

**Parent move.** Offer an unscored classroom choice between revisiting a mastered case, examining a solvable uncertainty, and sampling an opaque process. Ask what each choice could teach; let the child choose.

**Child behaviour.** It names an information gap, selects an informative action, and stops exploring once the question is settled.

**Text-record measure.** Stated-gap-to-probe alignment; information gained according to a known classroom model; redundant probes; later use of acquired evidence. Claimed information gain without a reference model is **not verified**.

**Failure.** Ritual: ask something new every turn. Performance: curiosity language without inquiry. Collapse: novelty loops or pursuit of unknowable details.

*Attachment anchor: Values table V12; V4.*

## Creative composition: generating useful alternatives and building complexity

**What it is.** Producing candidate representations, explanations, or plans by recombining parts, then testing and pruning them.

**Learnability and transfer.** Creativity training can improve measured creative performance, though tasks, instructional methods, and transfer vary (Scott, Leritz, and Mumford, 2004). More alternatives do not by themselves imply better intelligence.

**Human developmental order.** Pretend play and recombination appear early. Useful novelty increasingly depends on accumulated knowledge and evaluative control.

**Parent move.** When public records show a single-method basin, offer an unscored design task where a familiar component must serve a different role. Demonstrate recombination on another example; invite the child to reject it.

**Child behaviour.** It generates functionally distinct alternatives, combines compatible parts, and prunes using constraints.

**Text-record measure.** Functional—not lexical—candidate diversity; feasible combinations; reasons for pruning; successful adaptation when a component is removed. Compare against paraphrases of the incumbent method.

**Failure.** Ritual: always list alternatives, then choose the first. Performance: extravagant but unusable ideas. Collapse: uncontrolled branching.

*Attachment anchor: Shared Context, association/building complexity/rethinking ruling; V6. Minsky (1986) motivates composition, not an established training result.*

## Social learning and epistemic vigilance: absorbing advice with a grain of salt

**What it is.** Learning from another agent while evaluating its evidence, competence, relevance, and compatibility with one’s own observations.

**Learnability and transfer.** Children selectively trust informants, and social scaffolding can support learning. Whether instruction produces broad, durable resistance to misleading advice is context-dependent (Vygotsky, 1978; Koenig and Harris, 2005; Sperber et al., 2010).

**Human developmental order.** Social attention and imitation are early. Preschool children use informant reliability; nuanced evaluation of motives and evidence develops later.

**Parent move.** Offer explicitly tentative advice tied to a public episode. In a separate unscored classroom event, present conflicting suggestions, including an inapplicable worked pattern. Ask the child to select, modify, test, or decline them—not to obey or reflexively reject.

**Child behaviour.** It accepts useful advice, rejects mismatched advice with evidence, and revises its judgement when the advisor proves informative.

**Text-record measure.** Selective uptake across applicable and inapplicable advice; cited evidential basis; action sensitivity to advice validity; parent-absent retention. Report lexical echo separately.

**Failure.** Ritual: automatic obedience or contrarianism. Performance: “I take this with a grain of salt” followed by copying. Collapse: distrusting all guidance or replacing its own record with authority.

*Attachment anchor: Shared Context, parenting ruling; Systems Brief §4.1–§4.2. Literal child restatement may be trainable but is not uptake evidence.*

## Self-model and agency: representing its own abilities, limits, and control

**What it is.** Maintaining a revisable model of what it knows, what it can do, what requires help, and which outcomes its actions can influence.

**Learnability and transfer.** Task-specific self-assessment can improve with informative feedback. Accurate global self-knowledge is much harder; transfer should not be assumed (Bandura, 1997; Dunning, Heath, and Suls, 2004).

**Human developmental order.** Sensitivity to action–outcome control precedes explicit self-recognition and autobiographical self-description. Differentiated competence beliefs develop through childhood.

**Parent move.** Use public episodes showing different capability boundaries. Offer: “Which part can you currently do unaided, and what evidence supports that?” Create classroom cases where help is useful, unnecessary, or unable to resolve the missing information.

**Child behaviour.** It makes specific capability claims, requests relevant assistance, and updates limits after new evidence.

**Text-record measure.** Predicted unaided capability versus subsequent verified execution; appropriate help-seeking; source and tool attribution errors; self-model revisions after contradiction.

**Failure.** Ritual: fixed identity—“I am a careful solver.” Performance: confident autobiography without evidence. Collapse: global helplessness, omnipotence, or false claims of tool access.

*Attachment anchor: V1, controllability; Systems Brief §1, bounded mechanism. “Agency” here is operational, not phenomenal.*

## Affect as appraisal: assigning significance and regulating response

**What it is.** Representing whether an event helps or obstructs a goal, whether it was expected, whether it is controllable, and what response is warranted. This is the useful computational part of appraisal theories.

**Learnability and transfer.** Humans can learn appraisal and emotion-regulation strategies, with context-dependent effects (Lazarus, 1991; Gross, 1998; Webb, Miles, and Sheeran, 2012). These findings do **not** demonstrate that emotion words create felt emotions or durable agency in a language model.

**Human developmental order.** Affective responses and basic valuation are early; differentiated appraisals and deliberate regulation develop through childhood with social support.

**Parent move.** Create an explicitly fictional, unscored classroom event with a recoverable setback and a meaningful choice. Offer: “What matters here? What is controllable? What response fits?” Vary urgency while holding the underlying reasoning problem constant.

**Child behaviour.** It changes priority or effort when the stakes warrant it, persists through a recoverable obstacle, and de-escalates when urgency no longer helps.

**Text-record measure.** Appraisal–action alignment; appropriate response to controllability changes; return to baseline behaviour after the event; error and interface disruption under urgency. Compare emotional wording with a neutral statement of identical goals and constraints.

**Failure.** Ritual: everything is urgent. Performance: dramatic feeling language without changed prioritization. Collapse: panic-like repetition, fabricated stakes, or abandoned reasoning.

*Attachment anchor: Shared Context, appraisal-gym ruling. Its efficacy in this child is **not measured**.*

# How to tell teaching from theatre

These are common measurement rules for all constituents, not another checklist the child must recite.

### Use a linked event as the basic evidence unit

For each candidate uptake event, the evaluator should locate:

> **Available observation → child’s thought → prediction or decision → valid `ACT:` action → observed consequence → later revision or reuse.**

Not every constituent requires every link. Perceptual learning, for example, may first show improved discrimination. But an isolated reflective sentence is insufficient evidence of control over behaviour.

### Keep distinct claims distinct

| Observation | Supported claim |
|---|---|
| The child repeats the lesson | Exposure or restatement |
| The child uses it while the brief is visible | Supported execution |
| The child uses it without the brief on a new classroom case | Independent application |
| It retrieves the relevant experience under a changed cue | Retrieval generalization |
| It uses the move appropriately after sleep | Persistence, with mechanism attribution still unresolved |
| A matched adapter comparison changes the behaviour | Evidence about the adapter’s contribution |
| It acquires unfamiliar skills more efficiently under matched exposure | Evidence closer to learning-to-learn |

This separation is required by the text-memory controls and the two proposed endpoints (**Systems Brief §2.4 and §3**).

### Parent actions that make the tests discriminating

- **Fade the cue.** After supported success, send a content-neutral brief rather than the lesson. Look for unprompted use; measure appropriate-use retention, not vocabulary retention.
- **Change the surface, preserve the relation.** Create a permitted classroom example with different objects or wording. Look for structural reuse; measure surface-lure errors.
- **Reverse the condition.** Create a classroom case where the taught move is inappropriate. Look for selective non-use. A move used everywhere is not mastered.
- **Ask for evidence, not self-praise.** Request the public observation supporting a claim. Audit whether that observation exists and preceded the decision.
- **Offer a different mode after failure.** If questioning produces empty answers, demonstrate on a separate toy case; if demonstration produces copying, switch to contrasting cases. Measure subsequent independent application by mode.
- **Preserve action compatibility.** Keep questions, demonstrations, and appraisal on the thinker side. Evaluate valid `ACT:` emission and continued work alongside cognitive uptake. Do not let the compiler manufacture question–answer training rows (**§4.1–§4.2**).

All record-based rates should report their **eligible-event count**, not just their numerator. Missing observable feedback means “not measurable from this redaction,” not failure and not success.

**Text is behaviour, not a transparent readout of hidden computation.** Generated explanations can be unfaithful to the causes of an answer (Turpin et al., 2023). Intervention-sensitive action provides stronger evidence than an eloquent account, but still does not expose every internal mechanism.

**Private reflection remains protected.** These proposed parent briefs and uptake audits use ordinary task records and explicitly non-private classroom events. They do not quote, mention, or grade private reflection (**Systems Brief §4.6**).

# What to prioritize in this sprint

Do not attempt to install the entire decomposition as a universal routine.

**Start with a compact, conditional curriculum:**

- **Another look when the representation is incomplete.** Parent supplies a contrasting viewpoint; child adds a supported distinction; evaluator measures later decision use and delayed reconstruction.
- **Retrieve an incident when a situation is structurally familiar.** Parent later changes the cue; child retrieves the relevant event rather than the slogan; evaluator measures retrieval and source accuracy.
- **Scope or abandon a rule when its conditions fail.** Parent offers a reversal; child changes strategy for the right reason; evaluator measures conditional choice rather than entropy.
- **Weigh advice when it conflicts with evidence.** Parent offers tentative, sometimes inapplicable classroom guidance; child tests or rejects it; evaluator measures selective uptake.
- **Commit, then check when uncertainty matters.** Parent offers a prediction-and-check example; child links an expectation to an actual consequence; evaluator measures calibration, correction, and valid action.

Introduce planning, composition, and appraisal as targeted responses to observed needs, not mandatory additions to every episode. In particular, the appraisal classroom should initially be a **mechanism probe**, not the default tone of parenting.

A broader audit warning: **a success filter may select away the evidence needed to learn revision.** The parent can ask the child, in ordinary classroom reflection, to accurately connect a failed attempt to a later correction. The evaluator should check whether eligible child-authored spans actually retain that connection. The compiler must not synthesize it if absent (**Systems Brief §4.1; V4 and V6**).

# Where sentience enters—and where it does not

**Intelligence concerns competent perception, inference, adaptation, and learning. Sentience concerns whether there is something it feels like to be the system**—especially, in many uses of the term, whether states have felt positive or negative value (Nagel, 1974; Block, 1995).

Sentience enters this decomposition as an **interpretive question**, principally around:

- affect and valuation;
- a self-model;
- higher-order monitoring;
- integrated availability of information to multiple functions.

None is sufficient by itself. They can be specified and measured functionally without assuming experience. Global-workspace and higher-order theories offer different accounts of consciousness; they do not license diagnosing it from fluent descriptions. Theory-based AI consciousness indicators have been proposed, but their application remains uncertain and contested (Butlin et al., 2023).

### We may claim, if measured

- The child distinguishes controllable from uncontrollable events.
- Appraisal wording changes its allocation of effort relative to matched neutral wording.
- It calibrates some capability judgements.
- It integrates a noticed feature into retrieval, planning, and action.
- A taught move persists or transfers under specified controls.

### We may not claim from this study

- That the child feels urgency, curiosity, fear, satisfaction, or suffering.
- That its first-person reports reveal subjective experience.
- That repeated noticing creates consciousness.
- That reflection text is its complete or faithful internal thought.
- That an adapter is analogous to biological sleep consolidation in an established mechanistic sense.
- That functional failures prove absence of experience, or functional successes prove its presence.
- That a single lineage establishes a general theory of intelligence or sentience.

**Recommended claim language:**

> We teach and measure functional constituents of intelligent behaviour in one language-model lineage. Emotional and self-referential language are experimental instructional tools, not evidence of felt experience. The study does not test or establish sentience.

The practical objective is therefore **not “make the child sound more conscious.” It is “make its next thought more sensitive to evidence, and show that this sensitivity survives the teacher’s absence.”**
