## Abstract

Language agents can improve from deployment feedback through evolving textual
memory, batch experience distillation, and online low-rank parameter updates.
What remains unclear is whether a prior developmental intervention can improve
an agent's *later ability to learn* from its own experience, rather than only
its initial competence. We study one fixed, target-blind parent and one child.
On generated non-compiler tasks, the parent may select a closed process lesson
about prediction and revision, discriminating hypotheses, or scoping beliefs;
the child must apply the lesson through action, and public task outcomes alone
determine whether the resulting native continuation is eligible for a
low-rank write. The parent, its prose, and all nursery-only state are then
deleted. In a fresh compiler-optimization deployment, we cross parented versus
dose-matched unparented childhood with later personal writes enabled versus
disabled, while every arm retains the same evolving active-text mechanism,
tools, context, generated-token budget, and task opportunities. The primary
estimand is the root-level difference in write-enabled lifetime gain between
parented and unparented checkpoints; we call learning improved only when this
interaction, the absolute benefit of parented writes, and literal parented
lifetime improvement pass a fixed conjunctive test. [Across `N` independent
roots, the interaction was `D_RESULT`, parented write benefit was
`WP_RESULT`, and parented lifetime improvement was `L_RESULT`; `VALIDITY_RESULT`.]
Exact action--outcome provenance, adapter removal, target-binding
derangements, transactional write receipts, writer-yield mediation, and a
certified active-text reference localize the result. [The full developmental
package `PUBLIC_RESULT`.]
