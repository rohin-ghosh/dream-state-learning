# Upgrading the sleeping brain with targeted memory reactivation
Source: Trends in Cognitive Sciences 17(3):142-149, 2013. DOI: 10.1016/j.tics.2013.01.006 (not on arXiv)  
Authors: Delphine Oudiette, Ken A. Paller

## Abstract
A fundamental feature of human memory is the propensity for beneficial changes in information storage after initial encoding. Recent research findings favor the possibility that memory consolidation during sleep is instrumental for actively maintaining the storehouse of memories that individuals carry through their lives. The information that ultimately remains available for retrieval may tend to be that which is reactivated during sleep. A novel source of support for this idea comes from demonstrations that neurocognitive processing during sleep can benefit memory storage when memories are covertly cued via auditory or olfactory stimulation. Investigations of these subtle manipulations of memory processing during sleep can help elucidate the mechanisms of memory preservation in the human brain.

## Our differentiation
Claims: which memories consolidate is *steerable from outside* — presenting learning-associated cues (odors: Rasch et al. 2007; sounds: Rudoy et al. 2009) during SWS selectively boosts consolidation of the cued memories, with no awareness required. What survives long-term is what gets reactivated offline.
Mechanism: cue re-presented during SWS triggers reactivation of the associated hippocampal trace, pulling it into the replay/consolidation pipeline ahead of uncued competitors; content-addressable, item-level targeting.
Sleeper relevance: justifies an explicit consolidation-hint API — the waking thinker (or the user) can tag entries ("consolidate this", pin, boost) and the sleeper honors those cues when ordering its compile queue, alongside its autonomous novelty/reward priorities. TMR is evidence that selection and consolidation are decoupled stages — exactly the seam between thinker (tags/generates) and sleeper (compiles).
