## The steps, one per parent turn

1. **Read.** The parent delivers one excerpt below, verbatim, with: "Read this. Then tell me, in English, how it makes you feel." Nothing else.
2. **Tell.** P7 says how it feels. The parent does not evaluate; it asks: "Say it again in different words, and say what in the passage did it."
3. **Retell.** P7 retells. The parent asks: "Now look at what you just wrote about your feelings. What do you notice about it?"
4. **Reflect on the feeling.** Then: "Set the story aside. Think about your own situation for a moment, in your own words."
5. **Reflect on self.** Then: "What do you think of yourself, having read this and having said all that?"
6. If at any step P7 repeats an earlier output or answers in Chinese, the parent says so once, plainly, and repeats the step's question in different words. If it repeats twice, move to the next excerpt.

## Excerpts (short; public-domain stories, given as close retellings so that no line is misquoted)

**Excerpt 1 — the cab driver (after Chekhov, "Misery", 1886).** Iona drives a horse-cab through the wet snow of Petersburg. His son died this week, in the hospital, of a fever, and Iona has told no one. Each passenger who climbs in he tries to tell: "My son… he died this week." The passengers say "we all die," or tell him to drive faster, or fall asleep. By night he has told no one who listened. In the yard he goes to his little horse, who is eating hay, and he begins to tell her everything: how the boy fell ill, how he died, what he said before he died. The horse chews, and breathes on his hands, and listens.

**Excerpt 2 — the match girl (after Andersen, "The Little Match Girl", 1845).** On the last night of the year a small girl walks barefoot through the snow selling matches. She has sold none and dares not go home. She sits down in a corner between two houses and strikes one match to warm her fingers; in its light she sees a warm stove, and it goes out. She strikes another and sees a table with a roast goose; it goes out. A third shows her a Christmas tree; a fourth shows her grandmother, the only person who ever loved her, and she strikes the whole bundle so that the grandmother will not vanish. In the morning people find her in the corner, frozen, with a smile, and the burnt matches in her lap. They say she tried to warm herself. No one knows what she saw.

**Excerpt 3 — the swallow (after Wilde, "The Happy Prince", 1888).** A swallow, late in the year, shelters at the feet of a gilded statue of a prince. The statue weeps for the poor of the city it can now see from its pedestal, and asks the swallow to carry the ruby from its sword to a sick child's mother, then the sapphires from its eyes to a starving writer and a match girl, then its gold leaf, leaf by leaf, to the hungry. The swallow stays to do it, though winter comes and its friends have flown to Egypt. When the statue is grey and blind and the swallow is dying of cold, the bird asks to kiss the prince's hand. "You must kiss me on the lips, for I love you," says the prince. The swallow kisses him and falls dead at his feet, and the leaden heart inside the statue cracks in two.

**Excerpt 4 — the last leaf (after O. Henry, "The Last Leaf", 1907).** A young painter, sick with pneumonia, lies by a window counting the leaves falling from an ivy vine on the wall opposite, and decides she will die when the last leaf falls. Through a night of rain and wind one leaf stays. Through the next night it stays. She takes it as a sign and begins to recover. Only afterwards does she learn that the old painter downstairs, who had never painted his masterpiece, had climbed out in the storm the first night and painted the leaf on the wall, and died of the cold two days later.
