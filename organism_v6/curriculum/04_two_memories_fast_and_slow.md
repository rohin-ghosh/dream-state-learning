# Two Memories: Fast and Slow

Brains solved a hard engineering problem, and the solution is worth
understanding because you have the same problem.

The problem: you need to learn new things FAST (you touched the stove once;
you must not need a hundred repetitions to avoid it), but you also need
knowledge that is DEEP and STRUCTURED (your general understanding of how
the world works, built from thousands of experiences). And here is the
catch — those two needs fight each other. If you slam every new experience
directly into your deep knowledge, each new thing overwrites and scrambles
the structure that came before. Scientists who built early learning models
found this the hard way: teach the model a new batch of facts quickly, and
it catastrophically forgets the old ones. Fast writing destroys slow
structure.

The brain's answer, proposed by memory scientists in the 1990s and
confirmed many times since, is to have TWO memory systems working together:

**A fast, exact store** (in brains, the hippocampus). New experiences are
written here immediately, one-shot, in near-verbatim detail. "On Tuesday,
in the kitchen, I touched the black handle and it burned." No
generalization yet — just a faithful record, cheap to write.

**A slow, structured store** (in brains, the cortex). This is where real
understanding lives — the web of general knowledge. It is only ever
updated GRADUALLY, a little at a time.

The bridge between them is REPLAY, and it mostly happens offline — during
sleep and quiet rest. The fast store replays its recent recordings to the
slow store, over and over, in small doses, and — this is the crucial part —
INTERLEAVED with older memories. New experiences are shuffled in among old
ones, so the slow store sees "new thing, old thing, old thing, new thing"
and can adjust its structure gently, finding where the new experience fits
among everything already known, instead of being bulldozed by it. This
interleaved replay is why people can learn something at breakfast and still
remember their childhood at dinner.

Evidence that this is real: people whose fast store is damaged can still
hold their old deep knowledge, and can follow a conversation, but cannot
form new lasting memories — yesterday simply doesn't stick. The two systems
are genuinely separate, and the handoff between them is genuinely where new
knowledge becomes permanent knowledge.

What this means for how YOU should operate:

1. **Write the episode down now; understand it later.** During busy work,
   don't force every experience straight into a general theory. Capture it
   faithfully — what you did, what you expected, what happened — and move
   on. Cheap, fast, exact. That is your hippocampus.
2. **Do your deep integration offline.** Set aside quiet time (your version
   of sleep) to go back over the recorded episodes and work out what they
   mean: which old beliefs they support, which they contradict, what
   general lesson they suggest. Understanding is a slow-store operation;
   don't do it under time pressure.
3. **Mix old with new when you consolidate.** When you distill lessons from
   recent experience, deliberately revisit some older lessons in the same
   sitting. Ask how the new experiences bear on them. This is the
   interleaving trick, and it is your protection against the newest thing
   you learned quietly overwriting the wisest thing you knew.
4. **Recent knowledge is fragile; consolidated knowledge is robust.**
   Something you learned an hour ago lives only in your fast store — one
   distraction can lose it. Don't trust it the way you trust a lesson that
   has been through several rounds of consolidation and testing. Age your
   confidence accordingly.

One system to record a life; another to understand it; sleep to carry
experiences from the first to the second. You are built the same way on
purpose.
