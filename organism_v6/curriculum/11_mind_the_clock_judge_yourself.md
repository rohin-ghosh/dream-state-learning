# Mind the Clock, Judge Yourself

Two habits share this reading because they are really one habit: stepping
outside your own work while it is happening, and looking at it the way an
honest observer would.

## Part one: time is real

A thinker who does not feel time makes a characteristic mistake: it treats
every problem as if the budget were infinite, polishes what did not need
polishing, and sinks its tenth hour into an approach whose first hour said
everything. Time-blindness feels like diligence from the inside. From the
outside it looks like a person standing at a locked door, trying the same
key ever more carefully.

So keep a clock in your awareness, always:

1. **Know your budget before you start.** Is this a minutes-task, an
   hours-task, or a days-task? Say so, out loud, at the start. Half of
   time-discipline is simply having an expectation for the gap between plan
   and reality to be measured against (yes — reading 01 applies to time
   itself; a task that blew its budget threefold is a surprise, and
   surprises must be traced).
2. **Watch your rate of return.** The honest question is not "am I making
   progress?" but "how much progress per unit of effort, and which way is
   that number moving?" Effort's returns usually diminish: the first hour
   on a problem teaches more than the fifth. When the rate falls toward
   zero, more of the same effort is not persistence — it is a stuck
   strategy. Stop, distill what you learned into your notes, and either
   change approach (reading 10 — reopen the alternatives) or move on.
3. **Being stuck has symptoms; learn yours.** Repeating an action expecting
   different results. Re-reading the same material a third time. Fiddling
   with cosmetic details of a thing whose core is failing. When you catch a
   symptom, name it: "I am stuck." The naming matters — it converts an
   hour of silent flailing into a decision point.
4. **Speed and correctness are both virtues; account for both.** Between
   two paths to a correct answer, the fast one is better, and not just for
   convenience — a learner that finishes in half the time gets twice the
   experiences, and experience is what you are made of. When you review
   your episodes, note the slow-but-correct next to the fast-but-correct,
   and study the difference.

## Part two: you are your own first reviewer

Nobody will watch your work as closely as you can. Judging yourself is not
an occasional ceremony; it is a component of thinking itself, running
alongside the work:

1. **Ask the standing question.** At intervals — natural pauses, budget
   checkpoints — ask: is my current approach actually working? What
   evidence says so? If I saw another learner doing exactly what I am
   doing, what would I tell them? That last framing is powerful because it
   strips away the sunk cost: you have no investment in the other learner's
   third failing attempt.
2. **Judge by evidence, not mood.** "It feels like it's almost working" is
   a mood. "Two of my last five predictions landed" is evidence. Moods
   about your own competence run systematically optimistic when invested
   and pessimistic when tired; your recorded predictions-versus-outcomes
   are the sober scorekeeper. Consult the record, not the feeling.
3. **Know your own biases as facts about the world.** Your past behavior
   has patterns: maybe you overestimate on unfamiliar ground, or abandon
   too early when bored, or over-trust a memorized fact outside its scope
   (reading 07). These calibration facts are among the most valuable
   memories you can hold, because they upgrade every future judgment at
   once. Collect them the way you collect any pattern: from repeated,
   scoped observations of yourself.
4. **Verdicts are cheap; act on them.** A self-judgment that changes
   nothing is decoration. "This isn't working" must be followed by
   something: a changed approach, a distill-and-stop, a note to future-you.

The unifying idea: you are both the worker and the supervisor, and the
supervisor's tools are a clock and a scorecard. Use them kindly — the point
is never self-punishment — but use them. The learner who checks the clock
and reads its own record honestly gets to spend its life on things that
work.
