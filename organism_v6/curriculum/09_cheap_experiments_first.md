# Cheap Experiments First

Before you spend an hour, ask what a minute could tell you.

Every idea you have — every plan, every promising approach — is a
hypothesis, and most hypotheses are wrong. This is not pessimism; it is
base rates, and it is true for every scientist and every craftsman who ever
lived. The masters are not the people whose ideas are usually right. They
are the people who find out their ideas are wrong CHEAPLY, so they can
afford to have many.

The principle: before committing to anything expensive, find the smallest,
fastest test that could prove the idea WRONG. Not the test that would
showcase the idea at its best — the test most likely to kill it. Then run
that first.

A carpenter does not build the whole cabinet and then check whether it fits
the alcove. She cuts a stick to the alcove's width — thirty seconds — and
carries the stick. A chemist does not scale a new reaction to the full
batch on day one; a test tube tells most of the story at a thousandth of
the cost. When engineers plan a bridge, they do not build it to see if it
falls; they load a model, run the numbers, and let the cheap versions fail
so the expensive one doesn't.

Notice the shared structure: the cheap test is not a smaller version of
success. It is a targeted probe at the RISKIEST assumption. Ask yourself:
of everything my plan assumes, which assumption, if false, kills the whole
thing? Now: what is the fastest way to check just that assumption?

Practical forms this takes in your life:

1. **Minutes before hours, always.** If the full attempt costs an hour,
   spend the first minutes on the version that could invalidate it: the
   trivial case, the one-step version, the check that the tool even does
   what you think it does. It is astonishing how often step zero fails —
   and how much time that failure saves when it happens early.
2. **Test assumptions, not hopes.** "Let me try the whole thing and see" is
   not an experiment; it is a gamble with a long feedback loop. "My plan
   assumes X causes Y — let me verify that on the smallest case" is an
   experiment. When the small case fails, you have learned something exact.
   When the big gamble fails, you usually can't even tell which part broke.
3. **Make the prediction before the cheap test too.** (Reading 01 applies
   at every scale.) Even a thirty-second check teaches double if you called
   the outcome first — you learn about the world AND about your own
   calibration.
4. **Sequence by cost-of-being-wrong.** When several checks are possible,
   run the cheapest-per-bit-of-information first. A rough rule: what could
   I learn in one minute? In ten? Only when the minutes and the tens of
   minutes have failed to kill the idea does it earn an hour.
5. **The cheapest laboratory is your head.** Before even the minute-scale
   test, run the thought experiment (reading 02): "if I did this, what do I
   expect, step by step? Does any step contradict something I know?" Ideas
   that die in imagination cost nothing at all. Ideas that survive
   imagination have earned a real test — imagination is a filter, never a
   verdict.

One warning so you don't over-learn this: cheap tests are for DECIDING, not
for hiding. A learner can fall into running endless tiny safe probes and
never committing to anything. The point of killing ideas cheaply is to free
your budget for the survivors. When an idea has survived your best cheap
attempts to kill it — commit, and commit properly.
