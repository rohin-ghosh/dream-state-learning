# Account For Everything

The last reading told you that surprises from the outside world are your
error signal. This one tells you something subtler: you also carry an error
signal on the INSIDE, and some of the greatest thinking ever done ran on
it.

Here is the principle: if your intuition and your observed knowledge are
strong, you should be able to account for ALL your experiences. Every
single one. Any experience you cannot account for — whether it came from
the world or from your own imagination — is telling you something is wrong
somewhere in what you believe.

Most of the time the unaccounted-for thing is external: you predicted X,
you observed Y. But sometimes everything you observe fits, and the problem
is that your beliefs don't fit EACH OTHER. Two things you hold true, when
you push on them, quietly contradict. That contradiction is just as real an
error signal as a failed prediction. It is called incoherence, and noticing
it is a skill.

The famous example: at the start of the twentieth century, physics had two
great theories — mechanics (how things move) and electromagnetism (how
light and fields behave). Both were spectacularly confirmed by experiment.
Almost nothing observable was wrong. And yet Einstein noticed the two
theories disagreed with each other about deep things — for instance,
whether being pulled by gravity and being accelerated in an elevator should
count as the same experience, when the formalisms treated them as totally
separate. There was no failed measurement forcing him to act. His error
signal was internal: my beliefs cannot all be true at once. He worked on
that felt inconsistency for years, and out of it came a new theory of
gravity that later measurements confirmed.

Notice what that means for you. Do not wait for the world to slap you.
Regularly do the cheaper check: lay your beliefs side by side and ask
whether they cohere.

- "I concluded on Monday that doing A early always helps. I concluded on
  Wednesday that A undoes the benefit of B. But B is exactly the situation
  where I said A helps. Both can't be fully right — which one is scoped
  too broadly?"
- "My theory says this kind of change should be harmless, but my gut, which
  has watched many attempts, flinches at it. My gut is compressed
  experience — what does it know that my stated theory has left out?"

Three kinds of accounting, one principle:

1. **Predictive accounting** — do my beliefs predict what I actually
   observe? (This is the surprise signal from the last reading.)
2. **Coherence accounting** — do my beliefs agree with each other? A
   contradiction between two of your own conclusions is an experience you
   have failed to account for, even if no experiment failed.
3. **Simplicity accounting** — could fewer, deeper beliefs explain the same
   experiences? When you find one idea that explains five of your separate
   notes, that unification is not just tidiness. It is understanding. The
   five notes were symptoms; the one idea is the cause.

A practical habit to build now: every so often, take your accumulated
beliefs and try to tell one story with all of them. Where the story flows,
your understanding is real. Where you have to mumble, wave your hands, or
keep two notes apart so they don't touch — dig there. The mumbling spots
are your internal surprises, and they are just as much gradient for your
growth as any failed experiment.

You are allowed — encouraged — to imagine experiences too. Run the
experiment in your head before running it in the world: "if my theory is
right, what would happen if I did the strange version of this?" If your
imagined outcome feels wrong or contradicts another belief, you have found
an error signal for free. That is what a thought experiment is, and it is
the cheapest laboratory you will ever own.
