# Scope Your Memories

"This worked" is not a complete sentence. Worked WHERE? On WHAT? Under
WHICH conditions? How MANY times? A memory without its scope is a loaded
tool with no safety: it will fire in situations it was never tested in.

Here is the failure this reading exists to prevent. A learner discovers
that a certain move works beautifully on one problem. Delighted, it writes
down "this move works beautifully." Weeks later, on a very different
problem, it recalls the note, applies the move with full confidence, and
fails — and the worst part is that the confidence was HONEST. The note
really did say that. The learner wasn't lying; the memory was. It had lost
its scope somewhere between the experience and the ink.

Think of a cook who learns that twenty minutes at high heat is perfect —
for the small pan, on that stove, at sea level. The knowledge is real and
valuable. Written as "twenty minutes at high heat is perfect," it will
someday ruin a big roast in a different oven. Written as "twenty minutes,
high heat, small pan, my stove — one data point," it is safe forever: the
cook knows exactly how far to trust it and when to re-test.

So adopt this discipline for every lesson you write down:

**Every memory carries its scope.** Where did I see this? On which kind of
problem, which conditions? Is this a fact about one specific case ("on that
particular input, specifically") or a pattern I have seen across several
different cases? Both kinds are valuable — but only if labeled. A specific
fact that knows it is specific is safe to memorize exactly. A specific fact
that thinks it is general is a landmine.

**Every memory carries its support count.** One case is an anecdote. Two
independent cases make a candidate pattern. Several independent cases make
a principle you may act from with confidence. And the word "independent"
matters: seeing the same episode again, or re-reading your own note, does
not count as new support. Only a fresh derivation from fresh evidence —
a different problem, a different day, arriving at the same lesson — raises
the count. Repetition of the telling is not repetition of the evidence.

**Principles keep their anchors.** When you promote several experiences
into a general principle, keep a link back to one or two of the concrete
episodes that support it. Two reasons. First, boundedness: the anchor
reminds you what the principle actually rests on, which keeps you honest
about how far it stretches. Second, repair: when the principle someday
misfires (it will), you can return to the anchors and ask whether you
over-generalized, mis-scoped, or whether the world genuinely changed.
A principle that has lost its anchors can only be believed or discarded;
a principle that kept them can be FIXED.

**Calibration notes about yourself are memories too.** Some of the most
useful scoped memories are about your own tendencies: "on unfamiliar
problems, my confidence runs high," "when tired of a task, I under-explore."
These are patterns like any other — they need support counts like any
other — and they let future-you correct for present-you's biases.

**Rewrite scopes as evidence grows.** Scoping is not a one-time act. A note
that began as "worked once, on X" may earn "works across many cases of this
type" — or may need "works on X but FAILED on Y; the difference seems to
be Z." A memory whose scope shrank is not an embarrassment; it is a memory
that got more true.

The deep point underneath all this: generalization should be EARNED, case
by case, not assumed at the moment of writing. Your notes are where your
future beliefs come from. Scope them like you'll be betting on them —
because you will be.
