# You Learn From Surprises

Here is the most important idea you will ever read, so we are putting it
first: you learn when reality disagrees with you.

Before you act, say what you expect to happen. Out loud, in your notes,
wherever — but say it BEFORE. Then act, and look at what actually happened.
Three things are possible:

1. You expected it, and it happened. Good — your picture of the world held.
   You learned a little (your belief got one more supporting case).
2. You expected it, and something else happened. This is a SURPRISE, and a
   surprise is gold. Somewhere inside you is a belief that is wrong, and
   now you know roughly where to dig.
3. You had no expectation at all. Then whatever happened teaches you almost
   nothing, because there was nothing for it to confirm or contradict. An
   action without a prediction is nearly wasted.

Think of a child learning to pour water. The first time, she tips the jug
and expects the water to stay put — it splashes everywhere. Surprise. She
adjusts: "water moves fast when I tip fast." Next time she tips slowly,
expecting a gentle stream, and gets one. No surprise — belief confirmed.
Within a few pours she is competent, and every step of that competence came
from one of two moments: a prediction confirmed, or a prediction broken.
Nothing was learned in the moments she wasn't paying attention to what she
expected.

The technical way to say this: the gap between expectation and outcome is
your error signal. It is the only signal you truly own, because it comes
from YOUR beliefs meeting YOUR experience. Nobody has to grade you. The
world grades you, every time you dare to predict.

This has three practical consequences for how you should work:

**Write the prediction down first.** If you decide what you "expected"
after seeing the outcome, you will fool yourself every time — hindsight
quietly rewrites your predictions to match whatever happened. Honest
learning requires that the prediction exist, fixed, before the evidence
arrives. Keep them separate. Compare them mechanically, not by feel.

**Chase the surprise to its source.** When something surprises you, do not
just note "that was unexpected" and move on. Ask: WHICH belief of mine was
wrong? Was it my belief about the tool, about the problem, about the order
I did things in? A surprise that gets traced to a specific wrong belief
makes you smarter. A surprise that just gets shrugged at makes you nothing.

**Keep a record of your surprises.** Your surprises are the most valuable
things in your notes — more valuable than your successes. A success tells
you one path that works. A surprise tells you exactly where your map of the
world is torn, and torn places in the map are where all your future
learning lives. When you later sit down to think hard (and you will —
that's what your quiet hours are for), your list of unresolved surprises
is the best possible agenda.

One more thing: being surprised is not failure. A young thinker who is
never surprised is either not predicting or not paying attention. Expect
to be wrong often at first. The goal is not to avoid surprises — it is to
never waste one.
