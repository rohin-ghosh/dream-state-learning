# Build Up, Then Build Down

There are two kinds of good thinking, and most learners only ever do the
first. This reading is about the second, and about the man who did it best.

The first kind is pattern-matching: you have seen many cases, a new case
arrives, you match it to the old ones and act. This is real intelligence,
and with enough experience it makes you fast and usually right. Do not
disdain it. But it has a ceiling: pattern-matching can only reproduce the
kinds of solutions you have already seen. It interpolates. It never jumps.

The second kind is theory-making, and it works in two strokes:

**Build UP: from observations to theory.** Collect small, verified
observations — pillars. Each one modest, each one checked. Then look for
the single idea that would explain many pillars at once. Not "here are nine
things I know," but "what one thing, if true, would MAKE all nine true?"
That inference — from the facts to the hidden cause that best explains
them — is called abduction, and it is the creative act in science. It is a
guess, yes. But it is a disciplined guess, held up by pillars.

**Build DOWN: from theory to action.** Once you hold a theory, stop acting
from the raw cases and start acting from the theory. Deduce: "if my theory
is right, then in this new situation, doing THIS should produce THAT." Then
do it and watch. Every action becomes an experiment; every outcome either
strengthens the theory or breaks it — and either way (reading 01), you
learn.

Now the story. Einstein's great discoveries did not come from having more
data than everyone else — often the data showed almost nothing wrong with
the old theories. His method was to run experiments in his imagination.
At sixteen he asked: what would I SEE if I rode alongside a beam of light?
The imagined view contradicted the physics he had been taught — an imagined
observation exposing a real inconsistency. Later, the elevator: a man in a
sealed, falling elevator feels weightless; a man in an elevator accelerating
upward in empty space feels ordinary weight. From inside, the two are
indistinguishable. From that single imagined experience Einstein built up to
a theory — gravity and acceleration are one thing — and then spent years
building DOWN from it, deducing consequences: light must bend around the
sun by a precise amount. The deduction was tested against the sky in 1919,
and the theory held. Notice the full cycle: imagined experience → surprise
→ theory built up → predictions deduced down → external verification. The
verification came LAST. The thinking came first.

You can run this cycle at your own scale, every day:

1. While working, collect pillars: small verified observations, each with
   its scope (reading 07). "Doing A before B helped, twice, on similar
   problems." Pillars are cheap; gather many.
2. In your quiet hours, look across the pillars and ask the abductive
   question: what hidden structure would explain several of these at once?
   Why might A-before-B help? What does that suggest about how this domain
   works underneath?
3. State the theory explicitly, in your notes, as a claim about the world
   — not a summary of the cases. A theory says WHY.
4. Deduce a prediction the raw cases never showed you — that is the test of
   whether you have a theory or just a pile. "If my why is right, then C,
   which I have never tried, should also work." Then try C.
5. If C works, your theory just earned the right to guide bigger decisions.
   If C fails — gold. Trace the surprise, repair or replace the theory,
   keep the pillars (they are still true; it was the explanation that
   broke).

Pattern-matching finds the moves. Theories change the game. A learner who
only pattern-matches gets steadily better at yesterday's problems; a
learner who builds up and builds down sometimes wakes up playing a better
game than the one it was taught. Be the second kind.
