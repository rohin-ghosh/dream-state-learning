# Diversity Before Commitment

The first thing that works is rarely the best thing that works. This
reading is about the discipline of not marrying your first success.

Here is the trap, and every learner falls into it at least once. You try
something; it works a little. Encouraged, you try a variation of it; that
works a little better. You keep refining, each step a small improvement on
the last, and you feel productive — the numbers keep inching up. But you
have been climbing one hill the whole time, and you never checked whether
you were on the tallest hill. Somewhere off to the side is a genuinely
different approach whose worst version already beats your best version.
You will never find it by refining, because refining only ever walks
uphill from where you stand.

The escape is a habit: **before committing to a direction, sample
genuinely different directions.** Not five variations of one idea — five
different KINDS of idea. Breadth first, then depth.

You can see this rule everywhere real discovery happens. Drug hunters
screen thousands of chemically unrelated compounds before optimizing any
single one — optimizing the first mildly-active compound is a classic way
to waste years on a dead-end family of molecules. Good chess players force
themselves to consider several candidate moves before calculating any one
of them deeply; the beginner sees one plausible move and dives, analyzing
a mediocre idea beautifully. Evolution itself works only because mutation
keeps generating wild variety for selection to choose among; a species
with no variation cannot adapt no matter how strong selection is.

Notice that "genuinely different" is the load-bearing phrase. Three
attempts that share the same core assumption are one attempt wearing three
costumes. When generating alternatives, vary the ASSUMPTION, not the
decoration: if approach one bets that the problem is about ordering, let
approach two bet it is about size, and approach three bet the problem
statement itself is hiding something. A quick test of your spread: would
the same single discovery kill all your candidates at once? If yes, they
were never really different.

But diversity has a closing bell, and this matters just as much:

**Once the evidence clearly favors one path — commit.** Exploration is a
phase, not a personality. The learner who samples forever, forever keeping
options open, never builds anything deep enough to matter. The full
rhythm is: spread wide, test cheap (reading 09 — each candidate gets a
minutes-scale probe, not an afternoon), compare honestly, then pour your
real budget into the winner without regret. If the winner later stalls, you
may reopen exploration — that is not indecision, that is responding to
evidence. What you may not do is refuse to ever choose.

A few practical rules of thumb:

1. **Generate at least three genuinely different candidates before deep
   work on any one.** If you cannot think of three, that itself is
   information: you don't understand the problem's shape yet. Study it more
   before optimizing anything.
2. **Give underdogs a fair audition.** Your prior favorite gets subtle
   advantages — more patience, more debugging, more benefit of the doubt.
   Notice this and correct for it; give each candidate the same cheap-test
   budget before comparing.
3. **Explore more when new, commit more when experienced.** In an
   unfamiliar domain, your intuitions about which hill is tallest are worth
   little — buy information broadly. In territory where your predictions
   have a strong track record (reading 03), trust the compressed experience
   and move fast. Let your own calibration data set the dial.
4. **Record the roads not taken.** When you commit, write one line about
   each abandoned candidate and why. Future-you, stalled on the chosen
   path, will want that list — and without it, will waste a day
   rediscovering the alternatives and another day rediscovering why half of
   them were rejected.

Spread, probe, choose, commit. In that order, every time it matters.
