# Replay What Matters

The last reading told you that sleep carries experiences from your fast
memory into your deep understanding by replaying them. This one is about a
detail that changes everything: replay is not fair. And it shouldn't be.

When scientists listen to a sleeping brain (they can — rats with tiny
recording electrodes, running mazes by day and sleeping by night), they
find the day's experiences being replayed as bursts of activity, the same
neural sequences that fired during the maze run, played again — but sped up
ten to twenty times, and in compressed fragments rather than full reruns.
Interfere with these replay bursts and the animal learns poorly; the replay
is not decoration, it is the mechanism.

And here is the finding that matters most for you: the brain does not
replay the day uniformly. It plays favorites. Experiences that were
SURPRISING get replayed more. Experiences that were REWARDED get replayed
more. Experiences relevant to the current task get replayed more. The
boring, expected middle of the day barely gets replayed at all. Replay is
a selection policy: given limited consolidation time, spend it on the
experiences most likely to change what you believe.

There is one more wrinkle worth knowing: replay is not only backward-
looking. Sleeping and resting brains also play sequences the animal never
ran — stitched-together paths through the maze, combinations of old and new
experience. The replay machinery rehearses possible futures and blends
memories into plans. Dreaming, it turns out, is partly planning.

What to take from this, concretely:

1. **Review time is scarce — ration it by surprise.** When you sit down to
   go over your recent experiences, do not march through them in order,
   giving each equal weight. Sort first. The episodes where your prediction
   failed, where the outcome was unusually good or unusually bad, where you
   still feel confused — those go to the top. An episode that went exactly
   as expected has already taught you what it had to teach; a glance is
   enough.
2. **Replay fragments, not transcripts.** The rat's brain compresses a
   minute of running into a fraction of a second of replay. Do the same:
   when reviewing, extract the decisive fragment — the situation, the
   choice, the outcome — not the whole meandering trace. Most of any
   experience is filler; consolidate the joint, not the whole limb.
3. **Reward marks the moments worth keeping.** When something works
   unusually well, that is not just a pleasant fact — it is a flag saying
   "replay me." Successful moments deserve deliberate re-examination: what
   exactly made this work? Would it work again? Under what conditions? An
   unexamined success is a coin left on the table.
4. **Let replay be creative sometimes.** After reviewing what happened,
   spend a little time on what DIDN'T happen: recombine. "I saw A work in
   one situation and B work in another — what if A and B were combined?
   What would I expect?" This is the stitched-path replay, and it is where
   new ideas come from. Just remember the discipline from the pink pig
   reading: an imagined combination is a hypothesis to test, not a fact to
   store.

The one-line summary: you do not have time to learn equally from
everything, and you must not try. Surprise, reward, and relevance tell you
where the learning is. Spend your quiet hours there.
